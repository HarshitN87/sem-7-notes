    Unit Zero · Before We Begin

    Startups from Zero

    The basic ideas behind innovation and startups, explained with a college canteen app, plus how to crack an all-MCQ paper.

    Not a syllabus unit by itself, but every idea here (invention vs innovation, startup, customer, value proposition, revenue, equity, angel, VC, incubator, accelerator, MVP) appears in Units I–II and in the question banks. Read it once slowly. It takes about 20 minutes.

    Step 0.1Invention, innovation, entrepreneurship

    The big ideaAn invention is a new idea or thing. An innovation is a new idea that people actually use or pay for. An entrepreneur is the person who takes the risk of turning the idea into a working business.
    Example: the canteen queuePriya notices that the college canteen queue wastes 20 minutes of every lunch break.
    Invention: she writes an app that lets students pre-order food.
    Innovation: 2,000 students start ordering through it every day, and the canteen pays her ₹2 per order.
    Entrepreneurship: Priya quits her internship, puts in her savings and builds a company around it, accepting the risk it might fail.

    Innovation comes in different kinds. MCQs love these four:

    TypeWhat changesCanteen-app example
    ProductA new or better thing you sellThe pre-order app itself
    ProcessA new or better way of making or delivering itThe kitchen cooks in batches based on pre-orders
    Business modelA new way of making moneyCharging the canteen per order instead of charging students
    FrugalDoing more with far less: cheap, simple, good enoughA version that works by SMS for students without smartphones

    A related Indian word is jugaad: a clever, low-cost, improvised fix. Frugal innovation is the planned, scalable version of that spirit.

    New words
    Invention
    Creating a new idea, product or method
    Innovation
    Successfully putting a new idea into use: in the market or in society
    Entrepreneur
    A person who starts a venture and bears its risk
    Frugal innovation
    Creating good-enough, affordable solutions with limited resources

    Step 0.2What makes a startup different from a small business?

    The big ideaA small business (a tea stall) runs a known model in one place. A startup is searching for a new model that can grow very fast (scale), under extreme uncertainty about whether customers even want it. Most of Unit II (lean startup) is about reducing that uncertainty cheaply.
    Small businessStartup
    ModelKnown and proven (people will buy tea)Unproven; still being discovered
    Growth goalSteady, localVery fast, possibly national or global
    MoneyOwner's savings, bank loanOften outside investors who take a share of ownership
    RiskLowerHigh; most fail

    The lean-startup way to test an idea is to build an MVP (Minimum Viable Product): the smallest, cheapest version that lets real customers try it. For Priya, a Google Form plus a WhatsApp group before writing any app code. Then build → measure → learn, and either keep going (persevere) or change direction (pivot).

    New words
    Startup
    A young company searching for a scalable business model under uncertainty
    Scalable
    Able to grow revenue much faster than costs
    MVP
    Minimum Viable Product: the simplest version that can test the idea with real users
    Pivot
    Changing a key part of the plan based on what you learned

    Step 0.3How a business makes money

    The big ideaEvery business answers the same questions: who is the customer, what problem do we solve for them (the value proposition), how do we reach them, and do we earn more than we spend? The Business Model Canvas in Unit II is just these questions laid out as nine boxes.
    • Customer segment: who you serve. Priya: hungry students with short breaks, and the canteen owner.
    • Value proposition: why they choose you. "Skip the queue; food ready when you arrive."
    • Channels: how you reach them. The app, posters in the canteen, class WhatsApp groups.
    • Revenue streams: where money comes from. ₹2 per order from the canteen.
    • Cost structure: what you spend on. Servers, marketing, salaries.
    • Profit = Revenue − Costs.

    Common revenue models you'll see in MCQs: freemium (free basic version, paid extras), subscription (pay monthly), marketplace/commission (take a cut of each sale, as Priya does), and direct sales.

    New words
    Customer segment
    A group of customers with similar needs
    Value proposition
    The benefit that makes customers choose you
    Revenue model
    The way a business earns money (freemium, subscription, commission…)

    Step 0.4Where startups get money and help

    The big ideaStartups usually get money in stages. Early on it comes from the founders, friends and family, then angel investors, then venture capital (VC). In return for money, investors usually get equity: a share of ownership. Incubators and accelerators give help, space and mentoring.

    Scope. Funding stages (seed, angel, VC, Series A–D) are taught fully in Unit III, after the mid-sem. For the mid-sem you only need what Unit I says: angels come first with their own money, VC follows with larger amounts once there is traction, and incubators and accelerators support different stages (§ I.4).

    1. Bootstrapping: the founders use their own savings and early revenue. No one else owns a share.
    2. Friends and family: small amounts from people who trust you.
    3. Angel investors: wealthy individuals who invest their own money early, often in return for equity and with mentoring.
    4. Venture capital (VC): firms that invest other people's pooled money in larger amounts, in rounds (seed, Series A, B…), expecting fast growth.
    5. Government support: grants, schemes and funds (e.g. Startup India's Fund of Funds, managed by SIDBI).
    Example: equityAn angel gives Priya ₹20 lakh for 10% of her company. That values the company at ₹2 crore. If it later sells for ₹50 crore, the angel's 10% is worth ₹5 crore.
    IncubatorAccelerator
    Nurtures very early ideasSpeeds up startups that already exist
    Longer, flexible time (often 1–2 years)Short fixed programme in batches (cohorts), often 3–6 months
    Office space, mentoring, lab access; often run by collegesIntense mentoring, usually takes equity, ends with a Demo Day pitch to investors

    § I.1Understanding Innovation

    The big ideaInnovation = applying a new idea so that it creates value. It is not just inventing something. It counts as innovation only when people use it and someone benefits. "The market, not the laboratory, is the final judge."

    The definitions to recognise

    WhoWhat they said
    Peter Drucker (1985)Innovation is the specific tool of the entrepreneur: the means by which change is treated as an opportunity, not a threat.
    Oslo Manual, OECD & Eurostat (2018)A new or improved product or process that differs meaningfully from what the firm offered before and has been made available to users.
    Joseph Schumpeter (1934)Put innovation at the heart of capitalism. Coined creative destruction: new products and methods displace old ones and reshape industries (digital photography unseating film). The entrepreneur is the agent of this renewal.

    Both main definitions share one test: value in use. The slides put it as "Innovation = Application of ideas → Value creation", "not just invention, but market adoption", and "a continuous process, not a one-time event". In India, innovation often grows out of scarcity: small budgets, uneven infrastructure and price-sensitive buyers push founders towards affordable, simple, scalable answers.

    Invention vs innovation

    InventionInnovation
    Creation of a new ideaCommercial application of an idea
    Centred on the laboratoryCentred on the market
    High technical focusHigh customer focus
    May not create valueMust create value
    Example (from the notes)The smartphone image sensor was an invention. The innovation was putting a good camera in an affordable handset and linking it to the sharing and payment apps millions use. A patent can protect an invention, but a patent alone changes nothing in the market.

    The four types of innovation

    TypeWhat changesNotes' exampleSlides' example
    ProductWhat a firm offersTata Nexon EV (electric mobility adapted to Indian roads, prices, charging habits)Ola Electric scooters
    ProcessHow goods or services are produced and deliveredSwiggy's order-routing systemsAmul's supply-chain efficiency
    Business modelHow a firm creates and captures valueRazorpay: simplified digital payments for small merchants, earns per transactionPaytm: wallet → super app
    FrugalA useful outcome at low cost, under tight constraintsJaipur Foot (low-cost prosthetic limb)Jugaad solutions, Jaipur Foot

    The types overlap. UPI joined a technical standard with a business model that made small payments free for users yet attractive for banks.

    By degree: incremental innovation makes small, steady improvements (a new feature in a banking app every few weeks); radical innovation introduces something markedly different (the first low-cost smartphone that brought the internet to millions). Most firms rely on incremental gains, broken now and then by a radical leap. Both matter.

    Innovation as advantage, and innovation under constraints

    • For an established company, innovation is one route to advantage among several. For a startup it is often the only one, because it can't compete on scale, brand or capital.
    • Christensen (1997), The Innovator's Dilemma: new entrants win by serving overlooked customers with simpler, cheaper offerings, then improving until they challenge the leaders.
    • Radjou, Prabhu & Ahuja (2012) named the Indian pattern frugal or jugaad innovation: resourcefulness over abundance, treating constraints as design inputs rather than obstacles. Such solutions travel well because affordability matters in many markets.
    • The Global Innovation Index, published yearly by WIPO (World Intellectual Property Organization), records India's steady rise.

    § I.2Role of Innovation in India's Economic Growth

    The big ideaBetter products, processes and business models raise productivity, and across many firms and years that adds up to higher national income, more jobs and inclusion. India's startup story shows it happening.

    The economists behind the idea

    • Robert Solow (1957): much of output growth in advanced economies could not be explained by more labour or capital; the unexplained part came from technical progress.
    • Paul Romer (1990): put ideas at the centre of growth. Knowledge, unlike a physical good, can be used by many people at once without being used up.
    • C. K. Prahalad (2004): serving low-income customers, the bottom of the pyramid, is both a commercial opportunity and a path to development.

    The mechanism: a better process produces the same output with fewer inputs, freeing resources; a better product gives buyers more value for the same price. Summed across the economy, this raises national income.

    India's startup evolution (four phases)

    PeriodWhat shaped it
    1990sIT services: earned foreign exchange, built a large pool of technical talent
    2000sThe first wave of product startups
    2010sPlatforms and marketplaces (Flipkart, Paytm) reached mass markets as smartphones and cheap data spread
    2020sDeep technology, climate solutions, artificial intelligence and social-impact ventures

    Each phase drew on the one before. The constant was the recycling of talent: engineers from the 1990s services boom founded product companies, and early platform employees started their own ventures.

    The numbers to remember

    From the notes (DPIIT data)Recognised startups grew from about 500 in 2016 to more than 2.35 lakh by March 2026, reporting over 23 lakh direct jobs. India is the third-largest startup ecosystem in the world, with 100+ unicorns (private companies valued above $1 billion).

    Innovation for inclusion

    • UPI, built on public digital infrastructure, let a street vendor accept a ₹1 payment at no cost, bringing millions of small sellers into the formal economy.
    • The slides: Digital India was strengthened by process innovation (UPI) and product innovation (Aadhaar). Aadhaar + UPI let fintechs like PhonePe and Paytm reach remote villages. Frugal innovation empowers rural and semi-urban India.
    • Similar efforts improved access to credit, healthcare advice, and learning in regional languages.

    Measuring it, and its limits

    The Global Innovation Index scores inputs (e.g. research spending) and outputs (patents, high-tech exports). India now leads among lower-middle-income economies (WIPO, 2024). Resident patent filings are rising, and more startups are in tier-2 and tier-3 cities. Limits: job counts are self-reported and young-firm survival is uncertain. The innovation–growth link is not automatic: it depends on skills, finance, and a policy environment that lets new firms enter and weak ones exit.

    Case from the slides: UPI as an innovation driverLaunched by NPCI in 2016. Instant digital payments across banks. Boosted fintech startups (PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm) and made India a global leader in digital transactions. One of India's biggest process + ecosystem innovations: a platform on which other startups innovate, giving rise to several fintech unicorns (the digital platform model for innovation).

    § I.3The Entrepreneurial Mindset

    The big ideaAn entrepreneurial mindset is a learnable habit of thought: noticing opportunities, weighing risks carefully, and continuing when the outcome is uncertain. The slides sum it up as Creativity + Resilience.

    McGrath & MacMillan (2000): the mindset is a set of practices that can be learned (not fixed at birth), including readiness to act before all the facts are in. Recurring traits: eye for opportunity, tolerance for risk, creativity, resilience, and learning quickly from results.

    Causation vs effectuation (Sarasvathy, 2001)

    CausationEffectuation
    Starts with a fixed goal, then searches for the means to reach itStarts with the means at hand: who the founder knows, what the founder can do, and what the founder can afford to lose
    Suits predictable settings and detailed planningBuilds towards whatever goals those means allow. Experienced founders lean this way early on, because the future is too uncertain for detailed plans to pay off.

    The four mindset traits, one by one

    • Opportunity recognition: Shane & Venkataraman (2000) argued entrepreneurship is really the study of how opportunities are discovered, evaluated and acted upon. Prior knowledge helps: Rohan Verma saw farmers losing income to middlemen because he knew agriculture.
    • Creativity and problem-solving: durable ventures start from a problem the founder understands well, not a technology looking for a use. Urban Company grew from a plain frustration with finding reliable home services. Creativity is steady reframing, and it can be practised: widen the set of options before narrowing, and resist the first answer that works.
    • Risk-taking: Frank Knight (1921): risk can be estimated with probabilities; uncertainty cannot. Founders mostly face uncertainty, so they place small, informed bets. Myth: entrepreneurs love risk. Evidence: successful founders manage risk. The affordable-loss principle: commit only what you can lose without ruin, and treat the upside as a bonus.
    • Resilience and learning agility: absorbing shocks (lost funding, a key hire leaving, a copycat competitor) and continuing. Angela Duckworth (2016): grit = perseverance + sustained interest. Carol Dweck (2006): fixed mindset (ability can't change) vs growth mindset (ability improves with effort); growth-minded founders seek feedback and read setbacks as signals to improve.
    Resilience cases (slides)OYO: early struggles with customer trust. Flipkart: its failed "Ping" chat app, after which it refocused on logistics. Lesson: learn, adapt, try again. "Failure is information, not a verdict."

    Entrepreneurial challenges in India (slides): funding access beyond metros · regulatory complexity · infrastructure gaps (logistics, rural broadband) · balancing growth vs profitability.

    New words
    Effectuation
    Starting from the means you have (who you know, what you can do, what you can afford to lose)
    Causation
    Starting from a fixed goal and finding means to reach it
    Affordable loss
    Only commit what you can lose without ruin
    Grit
    Duckworth: perseverance plus sustained interest
    Growth mindset
    Dweck: belief that ability improves with effort

    § I.4The Indian Startup Ecosystem

    The big ideaNo venture succeeds alone. A startup ecosystem is the network of people, institutions and resources that helps new firms form and grow. Its strength comes from the density of links between players, not from any single player.

    Daniel Isenberg (2010) named the parts that must work together: supportive policy, access to finance, skilled talent, reachable markets, and a culture that respects entrepreneurship. Culture is the least visible part, and perhaps the most important: where failure carries shame, few people try. Role models help.

    • Leading hubs: Bengaluru (software, deep tech), Mumbai (fintech, consumer brands), Delhi-NCR, Hyderabad. Tier-2 cities such as Jaipur, Indore, Kochi have produced national-level ventures; recognised startups now exist in most districts. Digital tools loosened the link between location and opportunity.
    • A healthy ecosystem needs room for firms to close: thousands of recognised startups are marked dissolved, which is normal. What matters is that talent and capital recycle into the next attempt.
    • Capital enters in stages (details in Unit III). Angels come first, then VC once there is traction. Good investors also open doors to customers, later-stage funds and senior hires.

    § I.5National Policies on Entrepreneurship

    The big ideaPolicies don't replace market forces. They remove friction so a good idea faces fewer avoidable obstacles: lower compliance, tax breaks, early money, and public digital infrastructure.
    SchemeLaunched / run byWhat it does
    Startup India16 January 2016; recognition by DPIITThe central plank. Lighter compliance and self-certification under several labour and environmental laws; income-tax holiday for 3 years within the first 10; better access to finance and public procurement; faster, cheaper patent and trademark filing.     Atal Innovation Mission (AIM)Run by NITI AayogWorks "upstream", building an innovation culture before anyone founds a company.     Make in India2014Raise manufacturing's share of the economy and attract investment in domestic production; encouraged hardware ventures and electronics assembly.
    Digital IndiaSame era as Make in IndiaBuilt public digital infrastructure for identity, payments and data: Aadhaar, UPI and the account aggregator system, together called the India Stack. Transforms India into a digital society.
    Why the India Stack matters (notes)A new lending startup can verify identity through Aadhaar, collect repayments through UPI, and read consented financial records through the account aggregator system, without building any of it. Because these services are public, the fixed cost of starting a digital business falls sharply.

    Funding schemes

    • Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (2021): grants and early investment for ventures that can't raise money before they have a proven product.
    • Fund of Funds for Startups: managed through SIDBI; puts government money into VC funds, which then invest in startups.
    • Credit Guarantee Scheme: reduces the risk lenders face when lending to new firms.
    • State policies: grants, incubation space and procurement preferences. Karnataka, Telangana and Gujarat were early movers. Competition between states widens support.

    Limits of policy: it can't create demand for a weak product or substitute for a capable team. It lowers the fixed cost of trying.

    Policy snapshot (notes)Startup India lowers compliance and taxes. AIM builds innovation habits early. Make in India and Digital India supply manufacturing support and public digital infrastructure. Seed and Fund-of-Funds schemes address the early capital gap.

    § I.6Idea Generation Techniques

    The big ideaGood ideas are built, not received. Start with a sharp problem framed from the customer's side, then use a structured technique: brainstorming to widen the pool, design thinking to understand users and test cheaply, SCAMPER to improve something that already exists.

    Problem framing: ask what job the customer is trying to get done. "A person does not want a drill; the person wants a hole in the wall, and beyond that, a picture hung on it." A vague problem gives vague ideas.

    Brainstorming (Alex Osborn, 1953)

    • The core rule: separate generating ideas from judging them.
    • Stage 1: no criticism, quantity encouraged, wild ideas welcome, building on others' ideas is a virtue. Stage 2: judgment.
    • Works best with a mixed group and a skilled facilitator who keeps the stages apart; early criticism shuts down the flow. The slides: group ideation, no judgment, a burst of ideas in a short time.

    Design thinking (Tim Brown, 2009): five stages

    1. Empathize: observe and talk to users to understand real needs.
    2. Define: state the core problem clearly.
    3. Ideate: generate solutions, often by brainstorming.
    4. Prototype: turn the best ideas into rough, low-cost models.
    5. Test: put the models in front of users and gather feedback.

    The stages are not strictly linear; teams loop back as they learn. Strength: early, cheap contact with reality. The notes' example: a rural health-service team sitting with village families, finding that a feature they thought essential was useless and an overlooked need was the real opportunity.

    SCAMPER (Bob Eberle, 1971): seven prompts for improving what exists

    LetterPromptUniversity canteen example (slides & notes)
    SubstituteWhat part could be replaced?Replace ingredients or plastic packaging
    CombineWhat could be merged?Canteen + co-working / study space
    AdaptWhat idea from another field could be borrowed?Borrow ideas from other restaurants
    ModifyWhat could be larger, smaller or different?Healthy versions of meals
    Put to other useWho else could use it?—
    EliminateWhat could be removed?Long queues (pre-ordering), paper menus
    ReverseWhat could be turned around or reordered?Students cook; the canteen becomes a platform

    Slides' example: Ola Electric was born by adapting Ola's mobility expertise to EVs, which is SCAMPER: Adapt. The slides also list Blue Ocean Thinking: create uncontested market spaces.

    Which technique when?

    TechniqueBest for
    BrainstormingThe early, divergent phase, when you need volume
    Design thinkingWhen the real need is unclear and close contact with users will help
    SCAMPERImproving something that already exists

    They are often combined: design thinking to frame, brainstorming in the ideate stage, SCAMPER to refine the chosen concept. All favour quick, low-cost testing, which suits Indian ventures on tight budgets. Their shared ancestor is the lean startup loop of Eric Ries (2011): build, measure, learn.

    § I.7Case Studies and Frugal-Innovation Examples

    Case 1.A · Rohan Verma: a rural agri-tech ventureA 28-year-old engineering graduate from Uttarakhand returns home after four years in Bengaluru. He sees farmers facing unpredictable prices, poor market access and dependence on middlemen. Idea: a mobile platform linking farmers directly to buyers, with price transparency, logistics and crop advisory.     § II.1Market Opportunity Identification

    The big ideaIdeas are everywhere; opportunities are scarce. An idea becomes an opportunity only when three things meet: a real customer problem, a feasible solution, and a willingness to pay. Missing any one leaves you with just an idea.
    IdeaOpportunity
    AbstractMarket-validated
    Emotion-drivenEvidence-driven
    Unlimited in supplyScarce
    Centred on the founderCentred on the customer

    From idea to venture

    Why it matters, and the three tests

    Poor opportunity selection is the leading cause of startup failure: firms build what the market doesn't want, or enter at the wrong moment. Timing and market choice often matter more than the technology or the money raised. A well-chosen opportunity lowers execution risk, raises investor confidence, and makes scalable growth possible (while guarding against scaling before value is proven).

    TestQuestion
    Market needIs the problem painful and frequent, not a minor inconvenience?
    FeasibilityCan the team build and run it, technically and operationally, with its skills and the infrastructure available?
    TimingIs the market ready? This depends on outside factors: smartphone spread, data costs, regulation.
    Timing example (notes): online groceryTried around 2005: too early (thin internet, rare digital payments, few ready customers). The same idea after 2018, with cheap data, smartphones and trusted digital payments, supported large, fast-growing companies. "The idea had not changed. The timing had."

    Testing cheaply: confirm the problem by asking how people handle it today; check feasibility with a rough prototype or a manual version; test willingness to pay (the hardest) by asking for a commitment, such as a pre-order, a deposit or their time, because "talk is cheap". The slides add: "Market Need + Feasibility + Timing", build strong business models with moats, and "timing and execution separate success from failure" (examples: Urban Company, PharmEasy).

    § II.2Market Size Analysis: TAM, SAM, SOM

    The big ideaMarket size sets the ceiling on revenue, shapes investor attractiveness, and decides scalability. Describe it as three nested circles, and quote the smallest, most honest one (SOM) for your early market.
    • Quoting only TAM sounds naive; moving TAM → SAM → SOM shows you understand the constraints. SOM matters most in the early years.
    • Inflating the market (assuming everyone in India is a customer) loses credibility; a smaller, well-justified number carries more weight.
    • Investors often reject startups not because the idea is weak but because the market is too small.

    Top-down vs bottom-up

    Top-downBottom-up
    Starts from published industry data and narrows step by step (total food-delivery market → urban digital share → the share you can serve)Starts from a single customer: price per customer × number of customers you can reach, built upward (average order value × orders per month × customers in launch cities)
    Quick, but relies on broad figures that may not fit the ventureMore effort, but investors prefer it because it rests on the firm's own assumptions

    When both methods give similar figures, the estimate is more trustworthy.

    Two sizing mistakes: the one-percent fallacy ("we only need 1% of a huge market", which is very hard to win and shows the founder hasn't thought about reaching customers) and double counting (the same customers counted in several segments).

    § II.3Problem-Solution Fit & Customer Discovery

    The big idea"Fall in love with the problem, not the solution." Treat every belief about the customer as a guess, and test it by talking to real users (customer discovery). A venture must be desirable, feasible and viable at the same time.

    The best problems are: painful (real difficulty) · frequent (often enough to buy a fix) · costly (time or money) · underserved (existing options are poor). They come from the founder's own experience, from observing others, and best of all from both.

    Customer discovery

    MethodWhat it is
    InterviewsOpen questions about how people handle the problem today. Often the most revealing, because they surface reasons and feelings a survey misses.
    SurveysA fixed set of questions for a larger group
    ObservationWatch what people actually do, not what they say
    Pilot programmesA small group uses an early version

    It's a loop: hypothesis → test through interviews → feedback → refine the hypothesis → validation. In tier-2 and tier-3 cities, direct contact matters even more because habits differ from the metros.

    Interview rules: ask about past behaviour, not future intentions · avoid leading questions (they produce polite agreement) · listen more than you speak. "An interview is for learning, not for selling." The slides add: engage early adopters (beta testing), customer profiling, journey mapping, and test "will customers pay?". This reduces risk and helps pricing and positioning.

    Desirable, feasible, viable

    LensQuestionIf only this fails…
    DesirableDo customers genuinely want it?Cheap and profitable, but nobody wants it → fails
    FeasibleCan the team build and deliver it efficiently?Customers love it but it can't be built at a reasonable cost → fails
    ViableCan it make a profit and sustain itself?Loved and buildable, but loses money → fails

    Only where all three overlap is a venture sustainable. The three often pull against each other; the framework makes the tensions visible, and progress comes through small trade-offs. Use it as a checklist at every major decision.

    § II.4Lean Startup Methodology

    The big ideaEric Ries (2011): most startup effort is wasted building things customers don't want. So build a small version, measure how customers respond, learn, and repeat as fast and cheaply as possible. Speed through the loop matters more than perfection.

    Three habits: fast experimentation over long planning (test assumptions in weeks, not years) · customer feedback as the main source of direction · continuous learning as the goal of every activity.

    Vanity metricsActionable metrics
    Total registered users, page views: they can rise while the business goes nowhereConnect a change in the product to a change in customer behaviour: these are what a lean startup watches

    The Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

    The simplest version that still delivers value and lets the team learn. It is an experiment built to answer the riskiest question, not a careless low-quality product or simply a smaller version of the final vision. Its output is validated learning: knowledge confirmed by customers' behaviour.

    MVP typeHow it works
    Landing-page MVPDescribe the product on a web page and count sign-ups before building anything
    Concierge MVPDeliver the service manually, by hand, learning directly from early customers
    Prototype MVPA rough working model customers can try
    Case: Swiggy's MVPSlides: a website with 20 restaurants in Bangalore, orders taken by phone, delivery boys on bikes, then rapid iteration on user feedback. Today it is a nationwide delivery ecosystem. It tested demand and logistics before investing in elaborate technology.

    Pivot or persevere

    Every few cycles: persevere (keep refining and scaling) if the data show customers value it and the model is improving; pivot (change direction fundamentally, keeping what was learned) if demand stays weak however the product is refined. Decide on evidence, not emotion. A failed experiment is data, not defeat.

    Pivot examplePaytm shifted from a mobile-recharge service to a broad payments platform, a pivot that opened a far larger opportunity. (Notes' practice MCQ: this is an example of a pivot.)

    § II.5Business Model Development & the Business Model Canvas

    The big ideaA business model explains how a venture creates, delivers and captures value. The Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010) lays it out on one page in nine building blocks, so gaps and contradictions become visible.

    Business model ≠ revenue model. The revenue model is only the part that explains how the firm earns. The business model is the whole system: customers, offer, resources, partners, costs and revenue. A sound revenue model can sit inside a broken business model, for example when each customer costs too much to serve.

    BlockQuestion it answers
    Customer SegmentsWhich groups do we serve?
    Value PropositionsWhat do we offer to solve their problems?
    ChannelsHow does the offer reach them?
    Customer RelationshipsHow do we acquire, keep and grow customers?
    Revenue StreamsHow do we earn?
    Key ResourcesWhich assets does the model need?
    Key ActivitiesWhat must we do well?
    Key PartnershipsWhich outside relationships do we rely on?
    Cost StructureWhat are the main expenses?

    Order of filling: start on the right-hand side with customer segments and value proposition (the heart of the model), then work left to activities, resources and partners, and down to costs and revenue. This keeps the customer at the centre. Founders sketch several versions quickly before committing, and use the canvas to design new models and review existing ones.

    Case: Paytm's canvas (slides)Customer segments: urban youth, small vendors · Value proposition: easy digital payments, cashback offers · Channels: app, QR codes · Revenue streams: merchant fees, lending, insurance, ads · Key partners: banks, insurance firms. (The slides also show McDonald's canvas.)

    Adapting a model to India

    Four features pull a foreign model away from its template: price sensitivity · regional diversity (languages, tastes, incomes) · infrastructure gaps (logistics, connectivity) · regulatory complexity.

    Case: Ola vs UberUber: a global model with a premium focus. Ola: localised. It accepted cash, integrated auto-rickshaws, and offered regional-language apps, and gained market leadership through India-first innovations. "In India, localisation often decides success more than the underlying innovation."

    Distribution is often the hardest part to adapt; some ventures work through existing local networks (neighbourhood shops, community agents) instead of building their own.

    Challenges in building business models (slides): misjudging customer demand · copy-pasting Western models into India · balancing scale vs profitability · regulatory hurdles.

    § II.6Value Proposition Design & the Value Proposition Canvas

    The big ideaA value proposition says why a customer should choose you. The Value Proposition Canvas checks that what you offer (the value map) matches what the customer needs (the customer profile). When they line up, you have fit.

    A strong value proposition answers: what problem are we solving, for whom, and why is it better than the options they already have? The most common mistake: listing features instead of benefits. Translate every feature into what the customer gains, in the customer's words.

    Customer profile (the circle)Value map (the square)
    Customer jobs: functional, social and emotional tasks they're trying to doProducts & services: what you provide
    Pains: obstacles, frustrations, risksPain relievers: how the offer eases those pains
    Gains: outcomes and benefits they hope forGain creators: how the offer produces those gains

    The VPC complements the BMC: it zooms into two BMC blocks, customer segments and value propositions. It is more customer-focused, while the BMC gives the holistic view.

    Jobs are not only functionalA ride-hailing service sells transport (functional) but also the assurance of a fair price and a safe driver (social and emotional gains). A founder who only sees the functional job misses why customers really choose.

    Problem-solution fit: the three signs

    1. Customers recognise the problem on their own, without being persuaded.
    2. The solution addresses the most critical pain, not a minor one.
    3. Customers are willing to pay or switch.

    Scaling before fit just burns money faster. Fit is not a guarantee of success, but its absence reliably predicts failure. Beware imagined fit: polite words from friendly customers aren't evidence. Real fit shows in behaviour: customers who return, pay, and recommend without being asked.

    § II.7Revenue Models for Indian Markets

    The big ideaA revenue model is how a startup earns money. In India it must survive high price sensitivity, a large informal (cash) economy and low switching costs. Match the model to how customers actually use the product, and keep unit economics sound.

    Components: pricing (what each customer pays) · volume (customers or transactions) · customer lifetime value (total worth of a customer over the whole relationship) · scalability (revenue growing without matching cost growth). Balance them; don't optimise one alone.

    ModelHow it earnsExamples
    FreemiumBasic service free, advanced features paid; draws a big audience and converts a fractionByju's, Hotstar
    SubscriptionRecurring fee; predictable revenueCure.fit, Gaana+
    MarketplacePlatform connects buyers and sellers, earns a fee per transactionOla, Urban Company (UrbanClap)
    Direct sales / D2CBrand sells straight to customers, no intermediaries: keeps more margin and owns the relationshipboAt, Mamaearth
    Advertisement-basedFree to users; advertisers pay for attentionInshorts, Dailyhunt
    Fixed-feeSet price for a defined serviceRapido (per ride)

    Choosing: occasional, brief use → transaction fee · frequent, habitual use → subscription · large free audience with a small paying core → freemium or advertising. Many ventures combine models (a free ad-supported tier plus a paid subscription). A mismatch between revenue model and customer behaviour is a common, avoidable cause of failure.

    Case: boAt's direct-sales model (slides)Affordable audio and electronics for millennials. First sold via Amazon and Flipkart (low distribution cost), then built a strong D2C brand. Revenue: device sales + accessories.

    Challenges in the Indian context

    1. Growth vs profitability: low prices to win a price-sensitive market delay profit.
    2. Copying Western models without adapting them.
    3. Regulatory compliance adds cost and limits how revenue is earned.
    4. Unit economics (perhaps the most important): each transaction must eventually earn more than it costs. "Growth built on losing money per transaction is fragile… building on sand."

    Customer acquisition cost (CAC) vs lifetime value (LTV): if winning a customer costs more than that customer will ever be worth, growth only deepens the loss. Keep CAC well below LTV and watch the gap, because competition can raise CAC as you grow.

    § II.8Social Entrepreneurship & Frugal Innovation in India

    The big ideaSocial entrepreneurship solves a societal problem while staying financially self-sustaining (profit + impact). Frugal innovation (jugaad) is the method that lets such ventures serve price-sensitive, hard-to-reach customers profitably: affordable, simple, scalable.

    Social entrepreneurship

    Pure charitySocial enterpriseConventional startup
    Impact without a profit motive; depends on donationsDual aim: social mission + self-sustaining revenueProfit without a social mission

    Ventures sit on a spectrum: non-profits → social enterprises and hybrids → for-profit startups. The social mission often opens a market others ignored, and serving it well generates revenue.

    Indian exampleWhat it does
    SELCO SolarAffordable solar energy for rural households the grid didn't reach
    AmulCooperative model that returns value to the farmers who supply it
    Araku CoffeeLivelihoods for tribal farmers by linking their produce to premium markets

    The difficulty: two kinds of success. Financial success is easy to count; impact is hard to quantify. Serious ventures measure impact honestly, avoid over-claiming, and decide in advance which mission wins when the two conflict ("prevents drift"). India is fertile ground: many people are underserved in energy, health, finance and education. Slides' key question: can social entrepreneurship be as scalable as commercial ventures?

    Frugal innovation

    Three principles: affordability (reaches people with limited means) · simplicity (strip out features that add cost but not value) · scalability (spreads widely at low extra cost). The slides call it "part of our DNA (jugaad): necessity is the mother of invention", born of constraints with high impact on society.

    • Not mere cost-cutting: removing quality makes a worse product. Frugal innovation rethinks the product to deliver the needed outcome with far fewer resources.
    • Reverse innovation: a product designed for a poor market is later adopted in rich ones, because affordability and simplicity have value everywhere.
    • Examples: Jaipur Foot · Tata Nano (the ₹1 lakh car) · low-cost diagnostic devices · Grameen Bank (small loans to SHGs) · Agnibaan (Agnikul Cosmos, IIT Madras).
    Case: Jaipur Foot (slides)A low-cost prosthetic at $50 vs $8,000 globally. It restored mobility to millions and is recognised worldwide: an example of frugal + social innovation together.

    The link: social enterprises serve people conventional business overlooked (price-sensitive, hard to reach); frugal innovation supplies the method to serve them profitably. Together they form a distinctively Indian style of entrepreneurship.
