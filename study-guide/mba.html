    Unit One

    Foundations of Innovation & Entrepreneurship

    Types of innovation, the entrepreneurial mindset, India's startup ecosystem and policies, and techniques for generating ideas.

    Syllabus: understanding innovation (definitions; types: product, process, frugal, business model) · role of innovation in India's growth and startup ecosystem · entrepreneurial mindset · Indian ecosystem (incubators, accelerators, government bodies, angel networks, VC) · national policies (Startup India, Digital India, AIM, Make in India) · idea generation (brainstorming, design thinking, SCAMPER)

    Primer · Innovation

    Definitions to recogniseSchumpeter (1934): innovation is "new combinations" of resources: a new product, new method of production, new market, new source of supply, or new organisation. It drives creative destruction (1942), where new firms and technologies displace old ones. Drucker: "Innovation is the specific tool of entrepreneurs." OECD Oslo Manual: a new or improved product or process that differs significantly from previous ones and has been made available to users. Invention is the creation of a new idea; innovation is its successful commercial or social application.
    Type (syllabus)What changesIndian example
    ProductA new or improved good or service offered to customersOla Electric scooter; Paytm wallet
    ProcessA new or improved way of producing or deliveringAravind Eye Care's assembly-line cataract surgery; Dabbawalas' coding system; UPI rails
    Frugal"Doing more with less": cutting cost and complexity while keeping core value, for resource-constrained users (related: Jugaad)Jaipur Foot, Mitticool clay fridge, Godrej ChotuKool, Chandrayaan/Mangalyaan budgets, GE MAC 400 ECG
    Business modelHow value is created, delivered and captured (revenue logic)Jio's free-voice/cheap-data model; Zerodha's zero-brokerage discount model; Ola/Uber asset-light aggregation

    Other classifications that come up: by degree: incremental (small improvements) vs radical (breakthrough); disruptive (Clayton Christensen, The Innovator's Dilemma, 1997: starts at the low end or in a new market with a "worse" but cheaper or simpler product, then moves up-market) vs sustaining (better products for existing customers); architectural (Henderson & Clark: new arrangement of existing components); open innovation (Henry Chesbrough, 2003: using external ideas and paths to market); reverse innovation (Govindarajan: developed in emerging markets and then taken to rich markets).

    Primer · Role of Innovation in India

    • India is the world's third-largest startup ecosystem (after the USA and China), with more than a lakh DPIIT-recognised startups and 100+ unicorns. A unicorn is a privately held startup valued at ≥ US$1 billion (term coined by Aileen Lee, 2013); a decacorn is ≥ $10 billion; a soonicorn is a likely future unicorn.
    • Global Innovation Index (WIPO): India rose from 81st (2015) to around 40th (2023–24), the leading lower-middle-income economy.
    • Digital public infrastructure ("India Stack"): Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, ONDC, CoWIN. The JAM trinity is Jan Dhan + Aadhaar + Mobile.
    • Innovation drives GDP growth, jobs (startups create jobs rather than seek them), exports (IT services, pharma: "pharmacy of the world"), and inclusion (fintech, agritech, edtech in tier-2/3 "Bharat").

    Primer · The Entrepreneurial Mindset

    Trait (syllabus)Meaning

    Risk-takingCalculated risk, not gambling: acting under uncertainty with limited downside
    Problem-solvingTreating problems as opportunities; working back to root causes

    Also tested: McClelland's need for achievement (nAch); internal locus of control (believing outcomes depend on your own actions); growth mindset (Carol Dweck); effectuation (Saras Sarasvathy): start from the means you have ("bird in hand"), affordable loss (not expected return), "crazy quilt" partnerships, "lemonade" (use surprises), "pilot in the plane" (control rather than predict). Intrapreneurship is entrepreneurial behaviour inside an existing firm (term popularised by Gifford Pinchot).

    TechniqueKey facts

    Question Bank · Unit I

    Which economist described innovation as "new combinations" and introduced the idea of "creative destruction"?

    1. Peter Drucker
    2. Clayton Christensen
    3. Joseph Schumpeter
    4. Michael Porter
    Answer (c). Schumpeter defined innovation as new combinations of productive resources (1934) and described capitalism's "creative destruction" (1942). Drucker called innovation "the specific tool of entrepreneurs" but did not coin these terms. Christensen is known for disruptive innovation, and Porter for competitive strategy and the Five Forces.

    The key difference between invention and innovation is that innovation:

    1. Must always be patented
    2. Involves the successful commercial or social application of an idea
    3. Is always a physical product
    4. Happens only in R&D laboratories
    Answer (b). An invention is a new idea or device; it becomes an innovation when it is implemented and creates value for users or markets. Patents are optional, since many innovations (business models, processes) are not patented. Innovation can be a service, process or model, not only a physical product. It also happens in the field, in firms and through users, not only in labs.

    Aravind Eye Care's high-volume, assembly-line approach to cataract surgery, which cuts cost per operation dramatically, is primarily an example of:

    1. Process innovation
    2. Product innovation
    3. Radical technological innovation
    4. Open innovation
    Answer (a). Aravind's breakthrough is in how surgery is delivered (workflow, task specialisation, volume), which is a process innovation. The product (cataract surgery and lenses) existed before, so it is not product innovation. The technology is not new, so it is not radical technological innovation. It was developed internally, not through external collaboration, so it is not primarily open innovation.

    "Doing more with less", developing affordable solutions for resource-constrained customers by reducing cost and complexity, describes:

    1. Sustaining innovation
    2. Architectural innovation
    3. Luxury innovation
    4. Frugal innovation
    Answer (d). Frugal innovation strips a product to its essential value at a fraction of the cost (Jaipur Foot, Mitticool, ChotuKool). Sustaining innovation improves products for existing, demanding customers, usually adding features and cost. Architectural innovation reconfigures existing components into a new arrangement. "Luxury innovation" targets premium buyers, the opposite of frugality.

    Which of these is the best example of business model innovation?

    1. A faster processor chip
    2. A new flavour of biscuit
    3. Zerodha charging zero brokerage on equity delivery trades and earning through other fees
    4. A factory installing robotic welding arms
    Answer (c). Zerodha changed how value is captured (the revenue logic) in stock broking, which is business-model innovation. A faster chip and a new flavour are product innovations. Robotic welding is a process innovation inside manufacturing.

    According to Clayton Christensen, disruptive innovations typically:

    1. Begin by serving the most demanding, high-end customers
    2. Start at the low end or in new markets with simpler, cheaper offerings and later move up-market
    3. Are always based on breakthrough science
    4. Are launched mainly by market leaders
    Answer (b). Disruption starts where incumbents are not interested (low-end or non-consumers) with "good enough" products, then improves until it displaces the leaders. Serving demanding high-end customers is sustaining innovation. Disruption is about the business model and market entry, not necessarily breakthrough science. Incumbents usually fail to disrupt themselves (the innovator's dilemma), so disruptors are usually entrants.

    The concept of "open innovation", using external as well as internal ideas and paths to market, was popularised by:

    1. Henry Chesbrough
    2. Alex Osborn
    3. Eric Ries
    4. Steve Blank
    Answer (a). Chesbrough's Open Innovation (2003) introduced the idea. Osborn developed brainstorming. Ries wrote The Lean Startup. Blank created the customer development method.

    A "unicorn" startup is one that is:

    1. Profitable within its first year
    2. Listed on a stock exchange with a valuation of ₹1,000 crore
    3. Founded by a single founder
    4. Privately held and valued at US$1 billion or more
    Answer (d). Aileen Lee (2013) coined "unicorn" for private startups worth ≥ $1 billion. Profitability is not required, and many unicorns are loss-making. A listed company is no longer a private unicorn, and ₹1,000 crore is only about $120 million. The founder count is irrelevant.

    In terms of number of startups, India's ecosystem is generally ranked:

    1. First in the world
    2. Third, after the USA and China
    3. Tenth
    4. Fifth, after the USA, China, UK and Israel
    Answer (b). Government and industry reports (DPIIT, NASSCOM) describe India as the third-largest startup ecosystem. The USA remains first, so (a) is wrong. (c) and (d) understate India's scale.

    The "JAM trinity" that underpins much of India's digital innovation stands for:

    1. Jobs, Apprenticeship, Manufacturing
    2. Justice, Access, Mobility
    3. Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile
    4. Joint ventures, Angels, Mentors
    Answer (c). Jan Dhan bank accounts + Aadhaar biometric ID + Mobile phones made direct benefit transfers and fintech (UPI) possible at scale. The other expansions are invented distractors that sound plausible but are not the policy term.

    An entrepreneur's willingness to take risks is best described as:

    1. Taking calculated risks after assessing possible losses
    2. Gambling on outcomes with no information
    3. Avoiding all uncertainty
    4. Transferring every risk to investors
    Answer (a). Research on entrepreneurs (e.g. McClelland) shows a preference for moderate, calculated risk, where their skill influences the result. Gambling is uninformed risk. Avoiding all uncertainty makes entrepreneurship impossible. Investors share risk, but founders bear significant personal risk.

    The entrepreneurial trait of bouncing back from failures and setbacks is called:

    1. Creativity
    2. Locus of control
    3. Need for affiliation
    4. Resilience
    Answer (d). Resilience is recovering from setbacks and persisting. Creativity is generating new ideas. Locus of control is a belief about who controls outcomes. Need for affiliation (McClelland) is the desire for social relationships, which is not specifically entrepreneurial.

    An entrepreneur who believes that success depends mainly on their own actions rather than luck has:

    1. External locus of control
    2. Internal locus of control
    3. Fixed mindset
    4. High need for power
    Answer (b). An internal locus of control (Rotter) attributes outcomes to one's own effort, and is typical of entrepreneurs. An external locus attributes them to luck or others. A fixed mindset (Dweck) is the belief that abilities cannot grow. Need for power is about influencing others, not about causal beliefs.

    Saras Sarasvathy's effectuation principle of "affordable loss" means an entrepreneur:

    1. Invests only when the expected return is highest
    2. Borrows as much as possible to maximise upside
    3. Decides based on how much they can afford to lose, not on predicted returns
    4. Avoids partnerships to limit losses
    Answer (c). Effectuation replaces expected-return calculation (causal logic, option a) with a limit on acceptable downside. Heavy borrowing increases possible loss. Effectuation actually encourages partnerships (the "crazy quilt" principle), so (d) is wrong.

    Which is the most accurate distinction between an incubator and an accelerator?

    1. Incubators nurture early-stage ideas over a longer, flexible period; accelerators run fixed-term cohorts for existing startups, usually taking equity and ending with a Demo Day
    2. Incubators always take more equity than accelerators
    3. Accelerators only work with non-profit ventures
    4. There is no difference; the terms are synonyms
    Answer (a). Incubators (e.g. SINE, IIT Madras IC) support idea-stage ventures for 1–3 years, often with little or no equity. Accelerators (e.g. Y Combinator) run 3–6-month cohorts with seed money for equity and a Demo Day. (b) is backwards, since accelerators typically take equity. (c) is false. (d) ignores real structural differences.

    High-net-worth individuals who invest their own money in very early-stage startups, often also mentoring them, are called:

    1. Venture capitalists
    2. Private equity firms
    3. Commercial bankers
    4. Angel investors
    Answer (d). Angels (e.g. members of the Indian Angel Network, Mumbai Angels) invest personal capital at seed stage. VCs invest pooled money from limited partners, usually later and in larger rounds. Private equity invests in mature companies. Commercial bankers lend against collateral rather than taking equity risk.

    The Indian Angel Network (IAN) was founded in:

    1. 1991
    2. 2006
    3. 2016
    4. 2020
    Answer (b). IAN began in 2006, one of India's first organised angel networks. 1991 is the year of economic liberalisation. 2016 is the Startup India launch. 2020 is associated with PLI schemes and the pandemic.

    Which government body grants "startup recognition" in India under the Startup India initiative?

    1. Reserve Bank of India
    2. SEBI
    3. DPIIT (Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade)
    4. NABARD
    Answer (c). DPIIT (formerly DIPP) under the Ministry of Commerce & Industry recognises startups. RBI regulates banking and monetary policy. SEBI regulates securities markets (including AIFs, but it does not recognise startups). NABARD is the rural development bank.

    Startup India was launched on:

    1. 16 January 2016
    2. 25 September 2014
    3. 1 July 2015
    4. 15 August 2018
    Answer (a). Startup India launched on 16 January 2016. 25 September 2014 is Make in India. 1 July 2015 is Digital India. (d) is a distractor.

    Under DPIIT's definition, an entity can be recognised as a startup if, among other conditions, its annual turnover has not exceeded:

    1. ₹10 crore
    2. ₹25 crore
    3. ₹50 crore
    4. ₹100 crore
    Answer (d). The current definition (since 2019) is turnover ≤ ₹100 crore in any financial year and age ≤ 10 years from incorporation. ₹25 crore was the older (2016) limit, so option (b) may appear in older books and is a common trap. ₹10 crore and ₹50 crore were never the limit.

    The ₹10,000 crore "Fund of Funds for Startups" (FFS) under Startup India is managed by:

    1. NITI Aayog
    2. SIDBI
    3. State Bank of India
    4. IFCI
    Answer (b). SIDBI (Small Industries Development Bank of India) manages FFS, which invests in SEBI-registered Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs) that then invest in startups, so the government does not invest directly. NITI Aayog hosts AIM, not FFS. SBI and IFCI are not the fund managers.

    The Atal Innovation Mission (AIM) is housed under:

    1. Ministry of Finance
    2. Ministry of Education
    3. NITI Aayog
    4. Ministry of External Affairs
    Answer (c). AIM (2016) is NITI Aayog's flagship for innovation and entrepreneurship. The other ministries run other schemes. For example, the Ministry of Education runs Institution Innovation Councils through AICTE's innovation cell, which is a common confusion.

    Atal Tinkering Labs (ATLs) are set up in:

    1. Schools, to nurture curiosity and innovation among students of grades 6–12
    2. Only IITs and IIMs
    3. Defence research laboratories
    4. Rural panchayat offices
    Answer (a). ATLs give school students tools such as 3D printers, robotics kits and electronics. Universities host Atal Incubation Centres instead, so (b) confuses the two. Defence labs and panchayats are not ATL hosts; Atal Community Innovation Centres serve underserved regions.

    "Zero Defect, Zero Effect" and a lion made of cogs are associated with:

    1. Digital India
    2. Startup India
    3. Skill India
    4. Make in India
    Answer (d). Make in India (2014) aims to make India a global manufacturing hub. Its lion logo stands for strength, and "Zero Defect, Zero Effect" means quality with no environmental harm. Digital India focuses on digital infrastructure and services. Startup India covers recognition and funding. Skill India (2015) covers vocational training.

    Which is not one of the three vision areas of Digital India?

    1. Digital infrastructure as a core utility to every citizen
    2. Production-linked incentives for electronics exports
    3. Governance and services on demand
    4. Digital empowerment of citizens
    Answer (b). PLI schemes (from 2020) belong to the manufacturing push (Make in India / Aatmanirbhar Bharat). They are not a Digital India vision area. Options (a), (c) and (d) are exactly the three official vision areas.

    The nodal ministry for Digital India is:

    1. Ministry of Commerce & Industry
    2. Ministry of Communications only
    3. Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY)
    4. Ministry of Home Affairs
    Answer (c). MeitY coordinates Digital India. Commerce & Industry (via DPIIT) leads Make in India and Startup India. Communications (DoT) runs BharatNet, but it is not the nodal ministry for the whole programme. Home Affairs is unrelated.

    Which of the following is a benefit available to DPIIT-recognised startups?

    1. Income-tax exemption for 3 consecutive years out of the first 10 years (subject to eligibility under Sec 80-IAC)
    2. Permanent exemption from GST
    3. Guaranteed government contracts
    4. Automatic listing on the NSE
    Answer (a). Eligible startups (with Inter-Ministerial Board approval) get a 3-year tax holiday within the first 10 years. GST still applies. Public procurement is eased (relaxed prior-experience and turnover norms) but not guaranteed. Listing requires meeting SEBI/exchange norms (SME platforms exist, but listing is not automatic).

    The four basic rules of brainstorming proposed by Alex Osborn include all of the following EXCEPT:

    1. Defer judgement
    2. Go for quantity
    3. Encourage wild ideas
    4. Evaluate each idea immediately to save time
    Answer (d). Immediate evaluation kills creativity, so it is the opposite of Osborn's first rule (defer judgement). The four rules are: defer judgement, go for quantity, welcome wild or unusual ideas, and combine and improve ideas.

    In the Stanford d.school design-thinking model, the FIRST stage is:

    1. Define
    2. Empathise
    3. Ideate
    4. Prototype
    Answer (b). The order is Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test. Design thinking starts by understanding users' needs and feelings. Define (the problem statement) comes second. Ideate and Prototype come later.

    In design thinking, a "point-of-view" or problem statement is created during the:

    1. Empathise stage
    2. Test stage
    3. Define stage
    4. Prototype stage
    Answer (c). Define synthesises the empathy research into a user-centred problem statement ("User needs X because Y"). Empathise gathers the insights. Prototype builds cheap models. Test gets feedback on them.

    The three lenses that good design-thinking solutions should balance are:

    1. Desirability, feasibility, viability
    2. Speed, cost, quality
    3. Price, product, place
    4. Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities
    Answer (a). IDEO's framework: the solution must be desirable to people, technically feasible, and economically viable. (b) is the project-management "iron triangle". (c) is part of the marketing mix (4Ps). (d) is part of SWOT.

    In SCAMPER, the letter "P" stands for:

    1. Prototype
    2. Pivot
    3. Price
    4. Put to other uses
    Answer (d). SCAMPER = Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other uses, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange. Prototype is a design-thinking stage, pivot is a lean-startup term, and price is a marketing-mix element.

    A company adds a camera to a mobile phone. Which SCAMPER prompt does this best illustrate?

    1. Eliminate
    2. Combine
    3. Reverse
    4. Substitute
    Answer (b). Merging two products (phone + camera) into one is Combine. Eliminate would remove a feature (e.g. a bezel-less design removing buttons). Reverse would invert order or roles. Substitute would replace a component or material (e.g. a plastic body for metal).

    The SCAMPER technique was developed by:

    1. Tim Brown
    2. Edward de Bono
    3. Bob Eberle, building on Alex Osborn's checklist
    4. Alexander Osterwalder
    Answer (c). Eberle arranged Osborn's idea-spurring questions into the SCAMPER mnemonic (1971). Tim Brown (IDEO) popularised design thinking. De Bono created lateral thinking and the Six Thinking Hats. Osterwalder created the Business Model Canvas.

    Mansukhbhai Prajapati's "Mitticool", a clay refrigerator that needs no electricity, is a celebrated example of:

    1. Frugal (grassroots) innovation
    2. Disruptive platform innovation
    3. Luxury product innovation
    4. Financial innovation
    Answer (a). A low-cost, resource-light solution for rural users is frugal, grassroots innovation (recognised by the National Innovation Foundation). It is not a digital platform, not luxury, and not a financial product.

    "Jugaad" in the Indian innovation context most closely means:

    1. A formal R&D process with patents
    2. Imitation of foreign products
    3. A government subsidy
    4. An improvised, flexible, low-cost fix made with limited resources
    Answer (d). Jugaad (popularised globally by Radjou, Prabhu & Ahuja's Jugaad Innovation, 2012) means ingenious improvisation under constraints, and is an ancestor of frugal innovation. It is informal rather than formal R&D, it is creative adaptation rather than copying, and it has nothing to do with subsidies.

    Which Indian initiative gave rise to UPI, DigiLocker and Aadhaar-based e-KYC, together called "India Stack"?

    1. Make in India
    2. Digital India / digital public infrastructure
    3. Atal Innovation Mission
    4. Skill India
    Answer (b). India Stack is the digital-public-infrastructure layer promoted under Digital India (with NPCI, UIDAI and MeitY). Make in India is about manufacturing, AIM about innovation labs and incubators, and Skill India about vocational training.

    Innovation developed first for emerging markets and later adopted in developed markets is called:

    1. Sustaining innovation
    2. Incremental innovation
    3. Reverse innovation
    4. Closed innovation
    Answer (c). Vijay Govindarajan's reverse innovation, e.g. GE's low-cost portable ECG built for India and later sold in the US. Sustaining and incremental describe the degree of change, not the direction of flow. Closed innovation relies only on internal R&D.

    Which is the best description of a venture capital (VC) firm?

    1. A fund manager investing pooled money from limited partners into high-growth startups in exchange for equity
    2. A bank giving collateral-backed working-capital loans
    3. A government grant agency
    4. An individual investing personal savings in friends' businesses
    Answer (a). VCs (general partners) raise funds from LPs (pension funds, family offices) and invest for equity, expecting a few big winners. (b) describes debt financing. (c) describes grant schemes (e.g. SISFS, BIRAC). (d) describes angel or friends-and-family money.

    The Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS, 2021) mainly provides:

    1. Loans to large corporations
    2. Subsidies for farmers
    3. Tax refunds on exports
    4. Early-stage funding for proof of concept, prototypes, product trials and market entry, disbursed through eligible incubators
    Answer (d). SISFS (₹945 crore) gives grants of up to ₹20 lakh for proof of concept and prototypes, and up to ₹50 lakh (convertible debentures or debt) for market entry, through selected incubators. It does not lend to corporates, subsidise farmers or refund export taxes.

    In the entrepreneurial mindset, "problem-solving" as an opportunity lens means:

    1. Avoiding markets with problems
    2. Waiting for government to fix problems
    3. Treating customer pain points as potential business opportunities and working to their root causes
    4. Solving only technical coding problems
    Answer (c). Entrepreneurs see unmet needs (e.g. unreliable cab availability → Ola) as openings. Avoiding problems (a) or waiting for the government (b) is the opposite. (d) is too narrow, since problems can be social, logistical or financial.

    Which pair correctly matches an Indian incubator with its host institution?

    1. SINE – IIM Ahmedabad
    2. CIIE.CO – IIM Ahmedabad
    3. T-Hub – IIT Delhi
    4. NSRCEL – IIT Kharagpur
    Answer (b). CIIE.CO (Centre for Innovation Incubation and Entrepreneurship) is at IIM Ahmedabad. SINE is at IIT Bombay; T-Hub is a Telangana government initiative in Hyderabad; NSRCEL is at IIM Bangalore.

    Unit Two

    Opportunity Identification & Business Model Development

    Sizing a market, testing a problem with real customers, building and learning lean, designing the business model, and choosing how to earn revenue in India.

    Syllabus: market opportunity analysis in the Indian context · problem-solution fit and customer discovery · lean startup methodology · Business Model Canvas & Value Proposition Canvas · revenue models (freemium, subscription, marketplace, direct sales) · social entrepreneurship and frugal innovation in India

    Primer · Market Opportunity Analysis

    TAM Total Addressable Market = total demand for the product if 100% of the market bought it SAM Serviceable Available Market = the part of TAM your model and geography can actually reach SOM Serviceable Obtainable Market = the share of SAM you can realistically win soon (3–5 yrs) TAM ⊃ SAM ⊃ SOM

    Top-down sizing starts from an industry total and narrows down; bottom-up sizing multiplies (number of target customers × price × purchase frequency). Bottom-up is more credible to investors. Frameworks: PESTEL (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal), Porter's Five Forces (rivalry, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer power, supplier power), SWOT, and competitor mapping.

    Indian-context factors: high price sensitivity and value-seeking; a large young population; "India 1 / India 2 / India 3" consumer layers (from the Blume Indus Valley reports: a small affluent layer, a larger aspiring layer, and a vast layer with limited spending power); tier-2/3 "Bharat" growth; vernacular-first internet users; UPI and cheap data (Jio effect since 2016); cash-on-delivery culture; fragmented distribution (kirana stores); regulatory diversity across states.

    Primer · Problem-Solution Fit & Customer Discovery

    Steve Blank's Customer Development (The Four Steps to the Epiphany): Customer Discovery → Customer Validation → Customer Creation → Company Building. Motto: "Get out of the building". "No business plan survives first contact with customers." Customer discovery turns founders' hypotheses into facts through interviews: Is the problem real, frequent and painful? Who has it? How do they solve it today? Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test: ask about past behaviour, not opinions of your idea.

    Fit stageQuestion answeredEvidence
    Problem-solution fitDoes a real problem exist, and does our solution address it?Interviews, landing-page sign-ups, pre-orders
    Product-market fit (Marc Andreessen: "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market")Do enough customers want, use and pay for it?Retention curves flattening, organic growth, Sean Ellis test (≥ 40% "very disappointed" without it)
    Business-model / scale fitCan we acquire customers profitably?LTV : CAC ≥ 3, payback period

    Primer · Lean Startup

    Eric Ries, The Lean Startup (2011), built on Blank's customer development and Toyota's lean manufacturing. Core ideas:

    • Build → Measure → Learn feedback loop, as fast as possible (plan it in reverse: decide what to learn, then what to measure, then what to build).
    • MVP (Minimum Viable Product): the version with the least effort that allows a full turn of the loop and gives validated learning. Types: concierge MVP (manual service), Wizard-of-Oz MVP (looks automated, humans behind it), landing-page / smoke-test MVP, explainer-video MVP (Dropbox).
    • Validated learning: learning backed by real customer data, not opinion.
    • Pivot or persevere: a pivot is a structured course correction to test a new hypothesis (zoom-in, zoom-out, customer-segment, platform, business-architecture, channel pivots…).
    • Innovation accounting and actionable vs vanity metrics (total sign-ups is a vanity metric; cohort retention is actionable). A/B testing, the "five whys".

    Primer · Social Entrepreneurship & Frugal Innovation in India

    Social entrepreneurship applies entrepreneurial methods to social or environmental problems, measuring success by impact as well as, or instead of, profit. Triple bottom line (John Elkington, 1994): People, Planet, Profit. The term "social entrepreneur" was popularised by Bill Drayton (founder of Ashoka, 1980). Muhammad Yunus (Grameen Bank, microfinance, Nobel Peace Prize 2006) developed the "social business" idea. Forms in India: non-profit (Section 8 company, trust, society), for-profit social enterprise, cooperative, hybrid.

    Question Bank · Unit II

    The portion of the total market that a startup can realistically capture in the near term is called:

    1. TAM
    2. SAM
    3. SOM
    4. GMV
    Answer (c). SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) is the realistic near-term share. TAM is the entire demand, and SAM is the part your model and geography can serve. GMV (gross merchandise value) is the transaction volume on a marketplace, not a market-size tier.

    Estimating market size by multiplying the number of target customers × price × purchase frequency is:

    1. Bottom-up sizing
    2. Top-down sizing
    3. PESTEL analysis
    4. Porter's Five Forces
    Answer (a). Building up from customer units is bottom-up, which investors usually find more credible. Top-down starts from a published industry total and applies percentages. PESTEL analyses macro-environment factors, and Five Forces analyses industry competitiveness; neither is a sizing arithmetic.

    In PESTEL analysis, the "L" stands for:

    1. Logistical
    2. Local
    3. Labour
    4. Legal
    Answer (d). PESTEL = Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal. Logistics, locality and labour may appear within other factors, but they are not the named letter.

    Which is NOT one of Porter's Five Forces?

    1. Threat of new entrants
    2. Government subsidies
    3. Bargaining power of buyers
    4. Threat of substitutes
    Answer (b). The five forces are competitive rivalry, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer power and supplier power. Government policy is sometimes discussed as a factor affecting the forces, but it is not one of the five.

    A feature that especially distinguishes consumer markets in India is:

    1. Uniformly high disposable income
    2. Negligible use of digital payments
    3. High price sensitivity combined with rapid digital adoption in tier-2/3 cities
    4. A single common language across all users
    Answer (c). Indian consumers are value-seeking, while UPI and cheap data have driven fast adoption beyond the metros. (a) is false because income is highly stratified ("India 1/2/3"). (b) is false because India leads the world in real-time digital payment volume. (d) is false because linguistic diversity is why vernacular-first products matter.

    Steve Blank's customer development process has four steps. The correct order is:

    1. Customer discovery → customer validation → customer creation → company building
    2. Company building → customer creation → customer discovery → customer validation
    3. Customer validation → customer discovery → scaling → exit
    4. Ideation → prototyping → testing → launch
    Answer (a). Blank's sequence moves from finding a problem and customers (discovery), to proving a repeatable sales model (validation), to driving demand (creation), to scaling the organisation (company building). (b) and (c) scramble the order. (d) resembles design-thinking or product-development stages.

    "Get out of the building" is advice to founders to:

    1. Rent a larger office
    2. Work remotely
    3. Avoid investors until launch
    4. Talk to real potential customers instead of guessing their needs
    Answer (d). Blank's phrase means facts live outside, with customers, not in the founders' assumptions. It has nothing to do with office space, remote work or avoiding investors.

    "The Mom Test" (Rob Fitzpatrick) advises that during customer interviews you should:

    1. Ask people whether they like your idea
    2. Ask about their past behaviour and current problems rather than seeking compliments on your idea
    3. Interview only family members
    4. Pitch the product for the first ten minutes
    Answer (b). Even your mother will lie to be kind, so ask about specifics in their life ("When did you last…? How do you solve it now?"). Asking whether they like the idea (a) invites false positives. (c) misreads the title. Pitching (d) biases responses.

    Problem-solution fit is achieved when:

    1. The company becomes profitable
    2. The startup raises Series A
    3. There is evidence that a real, significant problem exists for a customer segment and that the proposed solution addresses it
    4. The product is available in all Indian states
    Answer (c). It is the first milestone: validated problem plus a validated solution concept (e.g. from interviews and pre-orders). Profitability (a) and Series A (b) come much later, around product-market or business-model fit. Geographic coverage (d) is a scaling outcome.

    The term "product-market fit" is most associated with:

    1. Marc Andreessen
    2. Alex Osborn
    3. John Elkington
    4. Joseph Schumpeter
    Answer (a). Andreessen popularised it: "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market". Osborn is linked to brainstorming, Elkington to the triple bottom line, and Schumpeter to creative destruction.

    The Sean Ellis test suggests product-market fit when at least what share of users say they would be "very disappointed" without the product?

    1. 5%
    2. 10%
    3. 25%
    4. 40%
    Answer (d). The heuristic threshold is 40%. The lower figures are distractors and would signal weak attachment.

    The Lean Startup (2011) was written by:

    1. Steve Blank
    2. Eric Ries
    3. Peter Thiel
    4. Alexander Osterwalder
    Answer (b). Eric Ries, who was Steve Blank's student and built on his customer development ideas. Blank wrote The Four Steps to the Epiphany. Thiel wrote Zero to One. Osterwalder created the Business Model Canvas.

    The core feedback loop of the lean startup is:

    1. Plan → Do → Check → Act
    2. Empathise → Define → Ideate
    3. Build → Measure → Learn
    4. Attention → Interest → Desire → Action
    Answer (c). Build–Measure–Learn turns ideas into products, products into data, and data into learning. PDCA (a) is Deming's quality cycle, (b) is design thinking, and AIDA (d) is an advertising model.

    A Minimum Viable Product (MVP) is best defined as:

    1. The version of a product that allows a team to collect the maximum validated learning about customers with the least effort
    2. The cheapest possible final product
    3. A product with all features but lower quality
    4. A beta version released only to investors
    Answer (a). This is Ries's definition: the MVP is a learning tool. It is not a cheap final product (b), nor a full feature set at low quality (c). It is aimed at real customers, not investors (d).

    Dropbox validated demand with a short video showing how the product would work, before fully building it. This is an example of:

    1. A concierge MVP
    2. Product-market fit
    3. A pivot
    4. An explainer-video MVP (smoke test)
    Answer (d). The video MVP drove beta sign-ups from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight, which validated demand. A concierge MVP delivers the service manually to a few customers. Product-market fit is a later outcome. A pivot is a change of strategy.

    A founder personally delivers groceries ordered over WhatsApp to 20 households to test demand before building an app. This is a:

    1. Wizard-of-Oz MVP
    2. Concierge MVP
    3. Freemium model
    4. Vanity metric
    Answer (b). A concierge MVP openly performs the service by hand for a few customers. In a Wizard-of-Oz MVP, customers believe it is automated while humans work behind the scenes. Freemium is a revenue model. A vanity metric is a misleading number.

    In lean startup terminology, a "pivot" is:

    1. Shutting down the company
    2. Raising a bigger funding round
    3. A structured course correction to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy or growth engine
    4. Hiring a new CEO
    Answer (c). A pivot keeps what was learned and changes one element (segment, problem, channel, model). Shutting down is not pivoting. Fundraising and CEO changes are not pivots in themselves. (Instagram pivoted from Burbn, and Paytm from mobile recharge to a payments and financial super-app.)

    Which is an actionable rather than a vanity metric?

    1. Week-4 retention of users who signed up in a given cohort
    2. Total cumulative app downloads
    3. Total page views since launch
    4. Number of social-media followers
    Answer (a). Cohort retention shows cause and effect and guides decisions. Cumulative downloads, page views and followers only go up and feel good, but they do not show whether the product creates lasting value.

    "Innovation accounting" in lean startup refers to:

    1. Tax accounting for R&D expenses
    2. Counting patents filed
    3. Auditing startup books for investors
    4. Measuring progress through learning milestones: establish a baseline with the MVP, tune the engine, then decide to pivot or persevere
    Answer (d). Ries's innovation accounting has three learning milestones (baseline → tune → pivot/persevere) to hold teams accountable for validated learning. It is not tax accounting, patent counting or audit.

    The Business Model Canvas was developed by:

    1. Eric Ries and Steve Blank
    2. Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur
    3. Michael Porter
    4. Clayton Christensen
    Answer (b). They introduced it in Business Model Generation (2010). Ries and Blank are lean startup and customer development. Porter is Five Forces and value chain. Christensen is disruption.

    How many building blocks does the Business Model Canvas have?

    1. 5
    2. 7
    3. 9
    4. 12
    Answer (c). Nine: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. 5 might be confused with Porter's forces, 7 with the 7Ps of services marketing, and 12 is a distractor.

    Which block sits at the centre of the Business Model Canvas?

    1. Value Propositions
    2. Customer Segments
    3. Key Resources
    4. Revenue Streams
    Answer (a). Value Propositions is in the middle, linking the infrastructure side (left) to the customer side (right). Customer Segments is at the far right, Key Resources is left-centre, and Revenue Streams is at the bottom right.

    Which BMC blocks make up the "efficiency" (left) side of the canvas?

    1. Channels, Customer Relationships, Customer Segments
    2. Revenue Streams and Value Propositions
    3. Customer Segments and Cost Structure
    4. Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, Cost Structure
    Answer (d). The left side shows how the business delivers efficiently (partners, activities, resources, costs). (a) and (b) are right-side (value) blocks. (c) mixes one block from each side.

    For Swiggy, restaurants and delivery partners as well as diners appear in the Customer Segments block. This makes it a:

    1. Niche market model
    2. Multi-sided platform
    3. Mass market model
    4. Segmented single-product model
    Answer (b). Multi-sided platforms serve interdependent groups, and each side's value depends on the others. A niche model serves one specialised group. A mass-market model treats one broad group alike. (d) does not capture the interdependence.

    In the Value Proposition Canvas, the customer profile consists of:

    1. Products, services, features
    2. Pain relievers, gain creators, channels
    3. Customer jobs, pains, gains
    4. Revenue, cost, profit
    Answer (c). The circle is customer jobs, pains and gains. The square (value map) holds products and services, pain relievers and gain creators, so (a) and (b) mix up the sides, and "channels" belongs to the BMC. (d) is a financial summary, not the canvas.

    "Fit" in the Value Proposition Canvas is achieved when:

    1. Pain relievers and gain creators address the most important pains and gains of the customer profile
    2. The product has more features than competitors
    3. Revenue exceeds cost
    4. All nine BMC blocks are filled
    Answer (a). Fit means the value map matches what customers really care about. More features (b) can add clutter without relieving pains. Profitability (c) and a filled canvas (d) are different milestones.

    In the VPC, "customer jobs" refers to:

    1. The employment status of customers
    2. Jobs the startup will create
    3. Tasks the company must perform
    4. What customers are trying to get done in their work or life: functional, social or emotional
    Answer (d). The "jobs-to-be-done" idea (Christensen / Ulwick): customers "hire" products to do jobs, e.g. "get to office on time" or "look professional". It is not about employment or company tasks; company tasks are "Key Activities" in the BMC.

    A music app offers ad-supported free listening and an ad-free paid tier with downloads. This revenue model is:

    1. Marketplace
    2. Freemium
    3. Direct sales
    4. Licensing
    Answer (b). A free base tier plus a premium upgrade is freemium (JioSaavn, Spotify). A marketplace earns commission between two sides. Direct sales sells your own goods. Licensing charges others for rights to use IP.

    The main revenue source of a marketplace such as Meesho, Flipkart or Urban Company is:

    1. Manufacturing margins on its own products
    2. Government grants
    3. Commission ("take rate"), listing and service fees, and advertising charged on transactions between buyers and sellers
    4. Interest income from customer deposits
    Answer (c). Marketplaces monetise the matching of two sides. Manufacturing margin (a) belongs to D2C or brands. Grants (b) are not a business model. Deposits (d) are a banking model.

    The biggest early challenge for a new two-sided marketplace is usually:

    1. The chicken-and-egg problem of attracting enough buyers and sellers at the same time (liquidity)
    2. Holding too much inventory
    3. Excessive manufacturing capacity
    4. Patent infringement
    Answer (a). Buyers will not come without sellers and sellers will not come without buyers. Pure marketplaces are asset-light, so inventory (b) and manufacturing (c) are not their main issues. Patents (d) are rarely the key constraint.

    boAt, Mamaearth and Lenskart selling their own branded products through their own websites and stores is an example of:

    1. Freemium
    2. Marketplace
    3. Subscription only
    4. Direct sales / direct-to-consumer (D2C)
    Answer (d). D2C brands sell their own products directly and keep the full margin and the customer data. They may also list on marketplaces, but their defining model is direct sales. Freemium and subscription describe pricing structures, not product ownership.

    The biggest risk specific to subscription businesses is:

    1. Inventory obsolescence
    2. Churn: customers cancelling their recurring plan
    3. Import duties
    4. Seasonal crop failure
    Answer (b). Recurring revenue only has value if customers stay, so churn directly erodes LTV. Inventory (a) matters more for product sellers. Duties (c) and crops (d) are industry-specific, not model-specific.

    A healthy startup is often expected to have an LTV : CAC ratio of about:

    1. 0.5 : 1
    2. 1 : 1
    3. 3 : 1 or higher
    4. It does not matter
    Answer (c). A lifetime value of at least three times the acquisition cost suggests sustainable unit economics. At 0.5:1 you lose money on every customer, and at 1:1 you only break even before overheads. Unit economics definitely matter to investors.

    "Sachet pricing", selling small, affordable units, suits Indian markets mainly because it:

    1. Matches low, irregular incomes and lets price-sensitive customers try or buy in small quantities
    2. Increases per-unit packaging efficiency
    3. Is required by law
    4. Only works for luxury goods
    Answer (a). From ₹1 shampoo sachets to small data packs, sachetisation lowers the entry price for "India 2/3". It usually reduces packaging efficiency, it is not mandated, and it is the opposite of luxury positioning.

    The "triple bottom line" concept (People, Planet, Profit) was coined by:

    1. Muhammad Yunus
    2. Bill Drayton
    3. C.K. Prahalad
    4. John Elkington
    Answer (d). Elkington coined it in 1994. Yunus founded Grameen Bank. Drayton founded Ashoka and popularised "social entrepreneur". Prahalad wrote The Fortune at the Bottom of the Pyramid.

    C.K. Prahalad's "Bottom of the Pyramid" (BoP) argument is that:

    1. The poor should be served only by charity
    2. The billions of low-income consumers are a large, profitable market if served with affordable, innovative business models
    3. Companies should focus only on luxury buyers
    4. Poverty cannot be reduced by business
    Answer (b). Prahalad argued that serving the BoP through frugal innovation can be both profitable and poverty-reducing. That directly contradicts (a), (c) and (d).

    SELCO India, founded by Harish Hande, is best known for:

    1. Microfinance for urban traders
    2. Clothing recycling
    3. Solar energy solutions with doorstep financing for the rural poor
    4. Mid-day meals
    Answer (c). SELCO combines solar lighting with tailored financing. Clothing recycling is Goonj (Anshu Gupta). Mid-day meals is Akshaya Patra. Microfinance is associated with SKS or Bandhan, not SELCO.

    Amul is an example of which social-enterprise form?

    1. A farmer-owned cooperative
    2. A government ministry
    3. A foreign multinational subsidiary
    4. A Section 8 charity
    Answer (a). Amul (the Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation, led by Verghese Kurien) is owned by millions of dairy farmers. It is not a ministry, not foreign-owned, and not a Section 8 not-for-profit company.

    Aravind Eye Care stays financially sustainable while treating most poor patients free or at very low cost mainly through:

    1. Foreign donations only
    2. Government ownership
    3. Charging all patients high fees
    4. Cross-subsidisation from paying patients plus high-volume, highly efficient processes
    Answer (d). Paying patients subsidise free ones, and assembly-line efficiency keeps unit cost very low. It is largely self-funded rather than donation-dependent, it is privately run, and it does not charge everyone high fees.

    ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan), completed for roughly ₹450 crore, is often cited as an example of:

    1. Luxury innovation
    2. Frugal engineering / frugal innovation
    3. Freemium pricing
    4. A marketplace model
    Answer (b). Mangalyaan reached Mars at a fraction of comparable missions' cost through reuse and lean design, which is "frugal engineering". It has nothing to do with luxury, pricing tiers or platforms.

    A key difference between social entrepreneurship and traditional (commercial) entrepreneurship is that social entrepreneurship:

    1. Never earns revenue
    2. Is always run by the government
    3. Treats social or environmental impact as the primary mission, with financial sustainability as a means
    4. Does not require innovation
    Answer (c). Social ventures put impact first but often earn revenue (SELCO, Aravind), so (a) is wrong. They are usually private or citizen-led, so (b) is wrong. They depend heavily on innovation, especially frugal innovation, so (d) is wrong.

    Which sequence best reflects the logical order of Unit II activities for a new venture?

    1. Market opportunity analysis → customer discovery (problem-solution fit) → MVP via Build–Measure–Learn → design the business model (BMC/VPC) and revenue model → scale
    2. Scale → MVP → customer discovery → market sizing
    3. Revenue model → exit → customer discovery
    4. Patent filing → IPO → market analysis
    Answer (a). Find a large enough opportunity, validate the problem with customers, test solutions lean, then define the business and revenue model before scaling. (In practice the steps iterate.) The other options put scaling, exits or IPOs before validation, which is how most startups fail.

    TAM, SAM, SOM?
    Total addressable ⊃ serviceable available (what your model and geography can reach) ⊃ serviceable obtainable (realistic near-term share).

    How does design thinking relate to lean startup?

    Business-model innovation: it changes how value is captured.
