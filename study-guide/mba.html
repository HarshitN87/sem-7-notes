    Step 0.1Invention, innovation, entrepreneurship

    The big ideaAn invention is a new idea or thing. An innovation is a new idea that people actually use or pay for. An entrepreneur is the person who takes the risk of turning the idea into a working business.
    Example: the canteen queuePriya notices that the college canteen queue wastes 20 minutes of every lunch break.
    Invention: she writes an app that lets students pre-order food.
    Innovation: 2,000 students start ordering through it daily, and the canteen pays her ₹2 per order.
    Entrepreneurship: Priya quits her internship, invests her savings and builds a company around it, accepting the risk it might fail.

    Innovation comes in different kinds. MCQs love these four:

    TypeWhat changesCanteen-app example

    FrugalDoing more with far less: cheap, simple, good enoughA version that works by SMS for students without smartphones

    A related Indian word is jugaad: a clever, low-cost, improvised fix. Frugal innovation is the planned, scalable version of that spirit.

    Step 0.2What makes a startup different from a small business?

    The big ideaA small business (a tea stall) runs a known model in one place. A startup is searching for a new model that can grow very fast (scale), under extreme uncertainty about whether customers even want it. Most of Unit II (lean startup) is about reducing that uncertainty cheaply.
    Small businessStartup
    ModelKnown and provenUnproven; still being discovered
    Growth goalSteady, localVery fast, possibly national or global
    MoneyOwner's savings, bank loanOften outside investors who take a share of ownership
    RiskLowerHigh; most fail

    The lean-startup way to test an idea is to build an MVP (Minimum Viable Product): the smallest, cheapest version that lets real customers try it. For Priya, a Google Form plus a WhatsApp group before writing any app code. Then build → measure → learn, and either keep going (persevere) or change direction (pivot).

    Step 0.3How a business makes money

    The big ideaEvery business answers the same questions: who is the customer, what problem do we solve (the value proposition), how do we reach them, and do we earn more than we spend? The Business Model Canvas in Unit II is these questions laid out as nine boxes.
    • Customer segment: who you serve. Priya: hungry students with short breaks, and the canteen owner.
    • Value proposition: why they choose you. "Skip the queue; food ready when you arrive."
    • Channels: how you reach them. The app, posters, class WhatsApp groups.
    • Revenue streams: where money comes from. ₹2 per order from the canteen.
    • Cost structure: what you spend on. Servers, marketing, salaries.
    • Profit = Revenue − Costs.

    Common revenue models: freemium (free basic, paid extras), subscription (pay monthly), marketplace/commission (take a cut), and direct sales.

    New words

    The benefit that makes customers choose you

    Step 0.4Where startups get money and help

    The big ideaStartups get money in stages: founders → friends and family → angel investors → venture capital (VC). In return for money, investors usually get equity: a share of ownership. Incubators and accelerators give help, space and mentoring.
    1. Bootstrapping: founders use savings and early revenue. No one else owns a share.
    2. Friends and family: small amounts from people who trust you.
    3. Angel investors: wealthy individuals investing their own money early, often for equity and with mentoring.
    4. Venture capital (VC): firms investing pooled money in larger rounds (seed, Series A, B…), expecting fast growth.
    5. Government support: grants, schemes and funds (e.g. Startup India's Fund of Funds, managed by SIDBI).
    Example: equityAn angel gives Priya ₹20 lakh for 10% of her company. That values the company at ₹2 crore. If it later sells for ₹50 crore, the angel's 10% is worth ₹5 crore.

    Primer 1.1 · Meaning and definition of innovation

    Definitions to recogniseDrucker (1985) called innovation "the specific tool of the entrepreneur" — the means by which change is treated as an opportunity rather than a threat. The Oslo Manual (OECD/Eurostat, 2018) defines innovation as a new or improved product or process that differs in a meaningful way from what a firm offered before, and that has been made available to users. Schumpeter (1934) described innovation as "new combinations" of resources — a new product, new method of production, new market, new source of supply, or new organisation — and in 1942 introduced creative destruction, in which new products and methods displace older ones and reshape entire industries. The classic example is digital photography unseating film.

    Both Drucker and the Oslo Manual point to the same test: value in use. The market, not the laboratory, is the final judge. Innovation is also purposeful: it responds to a need, a gap or an inefficiency. In the Indian setting, innovation often grows out of scarcity — limited budgets, uneven infrastructure, price-sensitive buyers push founders toward affordable, simple, scalable answers.

    Primer 1.2 · Invention versus innovation

    InventionInnovation

    Consider the smartphone camera. The image sensor was an invention. The innovation was the set of choices that placed a capable camera in an affordable handset and connected it to sharing and payment apps that millions now use.

    Primer 1.3 · Four types of innovation (with Indian examples)

    TypeWhat changesIndian example

    ProcessA new or improved way of producing or deliveringSwiggy's delivery routing; Aravind Eye Care's assembly-line cataract surgery; UPI rails

    These categories overlap. UPI, for instance, joined a technical standard with a business model that made small digital payments free for users and still attractive for banks.

    Other classifications that come up: by degree: incremental (small improvements) vs radical (breakthrough); disruptive (Clayton Christensen, The Innovator's Dilemma, 1997: starts at the low end or in a new market with a "worse" but cheaper/simpler product, then moves up-market) vs sustaining (better products for existing customers); open innovation (Henry Chesbrough, 2003: using external ideas and paths to market); reverse innovation (Govindarajan: developed in emerging markets then taken to rich markets); architectural (Henderson & Clark: new arrangement of existing components).

    Primer 1.4 · Innovation as a competitive advantage

    For an established company, innovation is one route to advantage among several. For a young venture it is often the only one — a startup rarely competes on scale, brand or capital early on. What it can offer is a better answer to a real problem. Sustained innovation lets a firm serve segments that incumbents ignore, lower its costs, and hold a position over time. Christensen (1997) described how new entrants win by serving overlooked customers with simpler, cheaper offerings, then improving until they challenge the leaders. Several Indian startups have followed this path in payments, logistics and education.

    Primer 1.5 · Innovation under constraints

    Radjou, Prabhu and Ahuja (2012) studied the Indian pattern and named it frugal, or jugaad, innovation. The approach favours resourcefulness over abundance: it asks what can be built with the materials, skills and money actually at hand, and treats constraints as design inputs rather than obstacles. Solutions that emerge this way tend to travel well, because affordability matters in many markets beyond India.

    Primer 1.6 · Innovation and economic development

    Economists have linked innovation to growth for a long time. Solow (1957) showed that a large share of output growth in advanced economies could not be explained by more labour or capital alone — the unexplained part came from technical progress. Romer (1990) placed ideas at the centre of growth: knowledge, unlike a physical good, can be used by many people at once without being used up. For a developing economy the message is direct — new products, better processes and smarter business models raise productivity and make room for higher incomes.

    The mechanism: when a firm adopts a better process, it produces the same output with fewer inputs, freeing resources for other uses. When it introduces a better product, buyers gain more value for the same price. Across many firms and many years, these gains add up to higher national income. Economies that generate and absorb new ideas grow faster than those that don't.

    Primer 1.8 · Employment and enterprise

    Startups now contribute to employment in a measurable way. According to DPIIT, the number of recognised startups grew from roughly 500 in 2016 to over 2.35 lakh by March 2026, and these ventures reported over 23 lakh direct jobs. Indirect employment — through suppliers, delivery partners and local services — adds much more. India now ranks as the third-largest startup ecosystem in the world and hosts more than 100 unicorns (private companies valued above US$1 billion).

    Additional figures from the slides: 12% CAGR over 5 years; 12,000+ active startups; 500+ incubators and accelerators; 750+ institutions; 2,500+ active angel investors; 4 million jobs. Hot sectors: fintech, e-commerce, medtech. Hot valuations (2024–25): Zerodha $8.2bn, Razorpay $7.5bn, Lenskart $7.5bn, Groww $7bn, Zepto $5.9bn.

    Primer 1.9 · Innovation for inclusion

    Growth alone does not guarantee that benefits reach everyone. Indian innovation has often aimed squarely at inclusion. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), built on public digital infrastructure and launched by NPCI in 2016, allowed a street vendor to accept a payment of a single rupee at no cost, bringing millions of small sellers into the formal economy. Similar efforts have improved access to credit, healthcare advice, and learning material in regional languages.

    C.K. Prahalad (2004), The Fortune at the Bottom of the Pyramid, argued that serving low-income customers — the "bottom of the pyramid" — is at once a commercial opportunity and a path to development. Many Indian ventures have tested that argument in the field. The JAM trinity — Jan Dhan bank accounts + Aadhaar biometric ID + Mobile phones — made direct benefit transfers and fintech at scale possible. UPI case study: launched by NPCI in 2016; enabled instant inter-bank digital payments; boosted PhonePe, Google Pay and Paytm; made India a global leader in digital transactions; often cited as India's biggest process + ecosystem innovation; created a platform for startups to innovate on top, giving rise to multiple fintech unicorns.

    Primer 1.10 · Measuring the contribution

    The Global Innovation Index (WIPO) scores countries on inputs (research spending) and outputs (patents, high-tech exports). India has climbed steadily — from 81st in 2015 to around 40th in 2023–24 — and now leads among lower-middle-income economies. Domestic measures tell a similar story: patent filings by Indian residents have risen, and the share of startups in tier-2 and tier-3 cities has grown, suggesting innovation is spreading beyond the traditional metro hubs.

    There are limits to what these figures capture. Job counts are self-reported, and the survival rate of young firms is uncertain. A careful reading looks past headline numbers to the quality of jobs and the depth of the technology. On both counts the trend has improved: a larger share of recent startups works in AI, clean energy and health, which demand advanced skills and pay accordingly. The link between innovation and growth is not automatic — it depends on skills, finance, and a policy environment that lets new firms enter and weak ones exit.

    Primer 1.11 · The entrepreneurial mindset

    McGrath and MacMillan (2000) described the entrepreneurial mindset as a set of learnable practices, not a fixed trait — including a readiness to act on a promising idea before all the facts are in.

    Trait (syllabus)Meaning
    CreativityGenerating novel, useful ideas and seeing opportunities others miss
    Risk-takingCalculated risk, not gambling: acting under uncertainty with limited downside
    Problem-solvingTreating problems as opportunities; working back to root causes
    ResilienceRecovering from failure; persistence; learning from setbacks

    Also tested:

    • McClelland's need for achievement (nAch) — entrepreneurs prefer moderate, calculated risk where their skill influences the outcome.
    • Rotter's locus of control — an internal locus attributes outcomes to one's own effort; typical of entrepreneurs.
    • Carol Dweck's growth mindset — ability improves with effort; feedback is sought, setbacks become signals to improve (contrast: fixed mindset).
    • Angela Duckworth's grit (2016) — long-term achievement = passion + perseverance.
    • Saras Sarasvathy's effectuation (2001) — the logic of expert entrepreneurs. Contrasts with causation (fixed goal → find means). Effectuation principles: bird in hand (start from means you have), affordable loss (decide by what you can lose, not predicted return), crazy quilt (partnerships), lemonade (use surprises), pilot in the plane (control rather than predict).
    • Frank Knight (1921) — risk can be estimated with probabilities; uncertainty cannot. Founders live mostly with uncertainty, so they place small informed bets and treat each result as data.
    • Gifford Pinchot — popularised intrapreneurship, entrepreneurial behaviour inside an existing firm.

    Primer 1.12 · Opportunity recognition, creativity & problem-solving

    Shane and Venkataraman (2000) argued that entrepreneurship is really the study of how opportunities are discovered, evaluated and acted upon. Prior knowledge helps a great deal: a founder who has worked in agriculture will spot problems in a village market that an outsider would miss. Rohan Verma's agri-tech venture (see case study) began exactly this way, from close observation of how farmers were losing income to middlemen.

    Entrepreneurs treat problems as raw material. The most durable ventures usually start from a problem the founder understands well, not from a clever technology in search of a use. Urban Company grew out of a plain frustration with finding reliable home services. Creativity, here, is less about sudden inspiration and more about steady reframing — asking what the customer is really trying to achieve, and whether there is a simpler way to help. The task is to widen the set of possibilities before narrowing it, and to resist the pull toward the first answer that happens to work.

    Primer 1.13 · Risk, uncertainty and failure

    Knight (1921) drew the distinction long ago: risk can be estimated with probabilities, uncertainty cannot. Founders live mostly with uncertainty, because they cannot know in advance whether a market will accept a new offering. The sensible response is to place small, informed bets rather than one large gamble, and to treat each result as data. Failure, viewed this way, becomes useful — it narrows the search and points to what should change next. Indian attitudes toward business failure have softened over the past two decades, though a stigma still lingers in many families and communities.

    A common myth holds that entrepreneurs love risk. The evidence points elsewhere. Successful founders tend to manage risk rather than seek it out, shaping early experiments so failure costs little and teaches a lot. The affordable-loss principle captures this: commit only what can be lost without ruin, and treat the upside as a bonus rather than a plan.

    Primer 1.14 · Resilience and learning agility

    Ventures rarely move in a straight line. Funding falls through, a key hire leaves, a competitor copies the product. Resilience is the capacity to absorb these shocks and continue. Duckworth's grit links long-term achievement to perseverance combined with sustained interest. Learning agility matters just as much: the founder who updates a plan quickly, on the strength of new evidence, tends to outlast the one who defends an early idea past the point of reason. Dweck's growth mindset shapes outcomes here — founders who hold it seek feedback rather than avoid it, and read a setback as a signal to improve rather than as proof of a limit.

    Primer 1.15 · The Indian startup ecosystem

    Isenberg (2010) identified the parts that must work together: supportive policy, access to finance, skilled talent, reachable markets, and a culture that respects entrepreneurship. When these connect, ideas move more easily from a notebook to a working company. Culture is the least visible part and yet may be the most important: where failure carries deep shame, few people will risk starting a firm; where it is treated as a normal step on the way to success, more will try. Role models matter — each visible success makes the path seem more possible to the next person.

    Primer 1.17 · Investors and mentors

    Capital enters a startup in stages. At the earliest point, angel investors put in their own money, and they often back a founder as much as a plan. Venture-capital funds follow with larger amounts once a business shows signs of traction. Money is only part of what these investors bring — a good investor opens doors to customers, later-stage funds and senior hires. Mentors, who may take no equity at all, offer something different: honest advice drawn from their own experience. Many founders credit a single mentor with helping them avoid a costly mistake.

    India now has organised angel networks — IAN (2006), Mumbai Angels, Chennai Angels, LetsVenture — which pool small investments from many individuals and share the work of assessing deals. At the larger end sit domestic and global venture funds, including Blume Ventures and Kalaari Capital alongside international firms with India offices. The presence of both local and foreign capital gives founders more options and, over time, better terms.

    Primer 1.19 · Idea generation — from problems to ideas

    Good ideas rarely arrive complete. They are built through a process that starts with a clear problem and moves through many rough attempts. The quality of the final idea depends a great deal on the quality of the problem statement. A vague problem produces vague ideas; a sharp problem, framed from the customer's point of view, tends to draw out sharper solutions.

    One helpful way to frame a problem is to ask what job the customer is trying to get done. A person does not want a drill; the person wants a hole in the wall, and beyond that, a picture hung on it. Framed this way, the real need becomes visible, and a wider set of solutions comes into view.

    Primer 1.20 · Brainstorming

    Alex Osborn (Applied Imagination, 1953) described brainstorming as a method for producing many ideas quickly in a group. It rests on a simple rule: separate idea generation from judgement. During the first stage no criticism is allowed, and quantity is encouraged, because a large pool of ideas is more likely to contain a strong one. Wild suggestions are welcome (they can be trimmed later), and building on others' ideas is a virtue. Judgment comes only in a second, separate stage. The method works best with a mixed group and a skilled facilitator who keeps the two stages apart.

    Osborn's four rules: defer judgement · go for quantity · welcome wild ideas · combine and build on ideas. Variants include brainwriting (6-3-5), reverse brainstorming, and mind mapping.

    Primer 1.21 · Design thinking

    Design thinking places the user at the centre. Tim Brown (2009), Change by Design, set out a version with five stages: Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test. The stages are not strictly linear; teams move back and forth as they learn. The strength of the approach lies in early, cheap contact with reality, which prevents a team from spending months on something no one wants.

    Also tested: the Double Diamond (UK Design Council) with four phases — Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver. And the "sweet spot" that balances desirability, feasibility and viability (IDEO).

    Primer 1.22 · SCAMPER

    Bob Eberle (1971) arranged Osborn's idea-spurring questions into the mnemonic SCAMPER: Substitute · Combine · Adapt · Modify (magnify/minify) · Put to other uses · Eliminate · Reverse/Rearrange. Each letter asks a question.

    Worked example: redesign the university canteen Substitute — replace plastic packaging with biodegradable material.
    Combine — canteen + co-working space.
    Adapt — borrow ideas from other restaurants.
    Modify — healthier versions of meals.
    Put to other uses — after-hours study space.
    Eliminate — long queues, paper menus.
    Reverse — students cook; canteen serves as a platform.

    Example from the slides: Ola Electric was born from adapting Ola's mobility expertise into EVs — a SCAMPER "Adapt".

    Primer 1.23 · Choosing a technique (and Blue Ocean)

    No single method fits every situation. Brainstorming suits the early, divergent phase, when a team needs volume. Design thinking suits problems where the real need is unclear and close contact with users will help. SCAMPER suits improving something that already exists. In practice the three are often combined: design thinking to frame the problem, brainstorming to fill the ideate stage, SCAMPER to refine a chosen concept.

    All three share a common ancestor in the lean startup's build–measure–learn loop (Ries, 2011). The shared logic: move quickly from idea to a rough test, put it in front of real users, and let their response guide the next step.

    Blue Ocean Thinking (Kim & Mauborgne) is also listed in the slides alongside these: creating uncontested market spaces (a "blue ocean") rather than competing in a crowded "red ocean". It is an idea-generation and strategy technique.

    Primer 2.1 · Idea versus opportunity

    IdeaOpportunity
    AbstractMarket-validated
    Emotion-drivenEvidence-driven
    Unlimited in supplyScarce
    Centred on the founderCentred on the customer

    The confusion between the two is costly. A founder who treats an unvalidated idea as a certain opportunity tends to commit money and time before there is evidence of demand. By the time the market delivers its verdict, the resources are already spent. The habit that guards against this: treat every belief about the customer as a guess to be tested, and spend as little as possible until the guess is confirmed.

    Primer 2.2 · Where an opportunity exists

    An opportunity exists only when three conditions meet at the same point:

    1. Real customer problem — one that people actually feel.
    2. Feasible solution — something a team can build with the resources at hand.
    3. Willingness to pay — a problem no one will spend money to solve is not yet a business.

    When any one of these is missing, the idea remains an idea. The path from idea to venture: Creativity → Validation → Design → Execution (Idea → Opportunity → Business Model → Venture). Skipping the validation step is the mistake that ends the most ventures.

    Primer 2.3 · Why opportunity identification matters

    Poor opportunity selection is the leading cause of startup failure. Studies of failed ventures point again and again to the same root: the firm built something the market did not want, or entered a market at the wrong moment. Timing and market choice often matter more than the technology itself or the amount of money raised. A well-chosen opportunity reduces execution risk, because the team is building toward a demand that already exists. It also raises investor confidence and makes scalable growth possible — while guarding against the danger of scaling a product before its value has been proven.

    Primer 2.4 · Market need, feasibility and timing

    A strong opportunity satisfies three tests:

    • Market need — the problem should be painful and frequent, not a minor inconvenience customers can easily ignore.
    • Feasibility — the solution must be workable in technical and operational terms, given the team's skills and available infrastructure.
    • Timing — the market must be ready. Readiness depends on factors outside the founder's control: smartphone spread, data cost, regulation.
    Example: timing in online groceryA venture that attempted online grocery delivery around 2005 was too early — internet access was thin, digital payments rare, few customers ready to buy food online. The same idea pursued after 2018 met a very different market: cheap data, wide smartphone use, trusted digital payments. The idea had not changed. The timing had.

    Testing the three conditions need not be elaborate. Confirm a real problem by asking potential customers how they handle it today and how much it troubles them. Check feasibility with a rough prototype, or even a manual version of the service. Test willingness to pay — the hardest of the three — by asking customers to commit something: a pre-order, a deposit, or their time. Talk is cheap; only a small commitment reveals true intent.

    Primer 2.5 · Market sizing — TAM, SAM, SOM

    TAM Total Addressable Market = total demand if 100% of the market bought it SAM Serviceable Available Market = the part your model and geography can reach SOM Serviceable Obtainable Market = the realistic near-term share you can win TAM ⊃ SAM ⊃ SOM

    A founder who quotes only the TAM tends to sound naive, because reaching the entire market is never possible at the start. A founder who can move from TAM to SAM to SOM shows an understanding of the real constraints on the business. The SOM, the smallest and most honest figure, is usually the one that matters most in the early years.

    Top-downBottom-up
    Starting pointPublished industry dataPrice × number of customers
    DirectionNarrow down step by stepBuild up from a single customer
    SpeedQuickMore effort
    Investor preferenceLess trustedPreferred — rests on the firm's own assumptions

    Indian food-delivery example: top-down might place the TAM at several lakh crore rupees, SAM at the urban digital population, SOM at a small initial share of 2–3%. Bottom-up would start from average order value, orders per customer per month, and number of customers the firm can sign up in launch cities. When the two methods point to similar figures, the estimate is more trustworthy.

    Two mistakes to avoidThe one-percent fallacy: claiming that capturing just 1% of a huge market will make the venture large. Winning 1% of a national market is extremely hard, and the claim usually reveals the founder hasn't thought about how customers will be reached. Double counting: counting the same customers in more than one segment, which inflates the total.

    IN PRACTICE: Quote the SOM, not the TAM, when you describe your early market. Investors trust a founder who narrows a large market to a realistic, defensible share far more than one who claims the whole of it.

    Primer 2.6 · Defining the right problem

    A common piece of advice: fall in love with the problem, not the solution. A founder attached to a particular solution will defend it even when customers reject it; a founder focused on the problem will happily change the solution until something works.

    The best problems share four features:

    • Painful — causing real difficulty.
    • Frequent — arising often enough that a solution is worth buying.
    • Costly — imposing a burden of time or money.
    • Underserved — existing options handle them poorly.

    Where do such problems come from? Often from the founder's own experience, since a problem felt firsthand is understood in a way a problem read about is not. They also come from close observation of others — watching where people struggle, improvise or complain. The richest source is usually a mix of the two: a problem the founder has both suffered and seen others suffer.

    Primer 2.7 · The customer discovery process

    Customer discovery is the practice of testing assumptions by talking to real users rather than guessing what they want. It rests on the recognition that a founder's beliefs about a problem are only hypotheses until customers confirm them.

    Steve Blank's Customer Development (The Four Steps to the Epiphany): Customer Discovery → Customer Validation → Customer Creation → Company Building. Motto: "Get out of the building". "No business plan survives first contact with customers."

    MethodHow it worksBest for
    InterviewsOpen questions about how people handle the problem todaySurface reasons and feelings a survey would miss
    SurveysFixed set of questions to a larger groupReaching scale; pattern confirmation
    ObservationWatch how people actually behaveCatching the gap between what people say and do
    Pilot programmesSmall group uses an early versionBehavioural, not just verbal, validation

    Discovery works as a loop: Hypothesis → Interview → Feedback → Refinement → Validation → sharpen the hypothesis → repeat. In Indian tier-2 and tier-3 cities, direct contact matters even more, because habits and constraints there often differ from those in the metros where founders tend to live.

    Rules for good interviews: ask about past behaviour rather than future intentions (what people have actually done predicts far better than what they say they will do). Avoid leading questions, which produce polite agreement rather than truth. Listen far more than you speak, resisting the urge to pitch. An interview is for learning, not for selling.

    Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test: even your mother will lie to be kind, so ask about specifics in their life — "When did you last…? How do you solve it now?" — rather than "Do you like my idea?"

    Primer 2.8 · Desirable, feasible, viable

    A sustainable venture must satisfy three conditions together:

    • Desirable — customers genuinely want it (discovery establishes this).
    • Feasible — the team can build and deliver it efficiently, given the technology and skills available.
    • Viable — it can generate a profit, so the business can sustain itself over time.

    Strength in one or two dimensions is not enough. A product customers love but that cannot be built at reasonable cost will fail. A product cheap to build and profitable but that customers do not want will also fail. Only where the three overlap does a sustainable venture become possible. The three dimensions often pull against one another; managing that tension is part of the founder's craft. The framework does not remove the tensions — it makes them visible, so they can be weighed rather than ignored.

    Primer 2.9 · Problem-solution fit & product-market fit

    Fit stageQuestion answeredEvidence
    Problem-solution fitDoes a real problem exist, and does our solution address it?Interviews, landing-page sign-ups, pre-orders
    Product-market fit (Marc Andreessen: "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market")Do enough customers want, use and pay for it?Retention curves flattening, organic growth, Sean Ellis test (≥ 40% "very disappointed" without it)
    Business-model / scale fitCan we acquire customers profitably?LTV : CAC ≥ 3, payback period

    Three signs of problem-solution fit: customers recognise the problem clearly without being persuaded; the solution addresses the most critical pain, not a minor one; and customers show willingness to pay or switch away from what they use now.

    One caution: fit can be imagined where it does not exist, especially when a founder is eager to proceed. Enthusiastic words from a few friendly customers are not the same as evidence — people are often polite. Real fit shows itself in behaviour: customers who return, who pay, and who recommend the product without being asked. A founder should look for these signs, and treat their absence as a reason to keep refining rather than to scale.

    Primer 2.10 · Lean startup methodology

    Eric Ries, The Lean Startup (2011), built on Blank's customer development and Toyota's lean manufacturing. The central insight: most of the effort a startup spends is wasted on products customers do not want. Three habits reduce that waste: fast experimentation over long planning; customer feedback as the main source of direction; continuous learning as the goal. These come together in the Build → Measure → Learn loop.

    The MVP (Minimum Viable Product)

    An MVP is the simplest version of a product that still delivers value to a customer and, more importantly, allows the team to learn. It is not a rough draft of the final product so much as an experiment designed to answer a specific question. Several kinds are common:

    MVP typeHow it worksExample
    Landing-page MVPDescribes the product on a web page; measures sign-upsGauges interest before anything is built
    Concierge MVPDeliver the service manually, by handFounder delivers groceries ordered over WhatsApp to 20 households
    Prototype MVPA rough working model customers can tryEarly product mock-up
    Wizard-of-Oz MVPLooks automated; humans work behind the scenesFake front-end, manual back-end
    Explainer-video MVPA short video showing how the product would workDropbox drove beta sign-ups from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight

    Swiggy's early MVP: a simple website, orders coordinated over the phone, deliveries arranged by hand. This bare version tested whether customers wanted food delivered and whether logistics could work — without the cost of building elaborate technology first. Only after demand was proven did the company invest in the systems that later allowed it to scale.

    What an MVP is not: not a low-quality product released carelessly, and not merely a smaller version of the final vision. It is a deliberate experiment, built to answer the riskiest question the venture faces, and no larger than that question requires. The value it produces is validated learning — knowledge about customers confirmed by their behaviour rather than assumed.

    Pivot or persevere

    Every few cycles, a startup faces a decision. It can persevere, continuing to refine and scale the current model, or it can pivot, changing direction in a fundamental way while keeping what it has learned. The choice should rest on evidence rather than emotion. If the data show customers value the product and the model is improving, perseverance is right. If the data show weak demand no matter how the product is refined, a pivot is wiser. Lean startups treat a failed experiment as data, not defeat.

    Paytm's pivot: shifted from a mobile-recharge service to a broad payments platform, opening a far larger opportunity than the original idea. Flipkart's pivot: the failed "Ping" chat app led to a refocus on logistics.

    Innovation accounting and metrics

    Ries's innovation accounting has three learning milestones: establish a baseline with the MVP, tune the engine, then decide to pivot or persevere. It holds teams accountable for validated learning, not just activity.

    A caution accompanies the emphasis on measurement. Not every number is worth tracking. Vanity metrics — total registered users, page views, cumulative downloads, social-media followers — can rise steadily while the business goes nowhere, because they do not show whether customers find real value. Actionable metrics connect a change in the product to a change in customer behaviour — cohort retention is the classic example. Other tools: A/B testing, the "five whys".

    Primer 2.11 · What a business model is

    A business model explains how a venture works as a system. It answers three linked questions:

    1. Create value — what does the firm offer, and to whom?
    2. Deliver value — through which channels and relationships?
    3. Capture value — how does it earn revenue and manage cost?

    A business model is the bridge between an innovation and the money it earns. Two firms can sell a similar product and yet succeed or fail depending on the model that surrounds it.

    A business model is not the same as a revenue model. The revenue model is only one part — the part that explains how the firm earns. The business model is the wider system: customers, offer, resources, partners, costs, and revenue. A firm can have a sound revenue model inside a broken business model (for instance, when the cost of serving each customer is too high). It is the whole system, not any single part, that decides whether a venture works.

    Primer 2.12 · The Business Model Canvas

    The BMC, developed by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur (Business Model Generation, 2010), gives a single-page view of a business through nine building blocks:

    BlockQuestionSwiggy example

    Customer RelationshipsWhat relationship does each segment expect?Self-service + support chat; Swiggy One loyalty

    The natural order of filling: most practitioners begin on the right side — Customer Segments and Value Propositions — because these are the heart of the model and everything else depends on them. Only once the firm is clear about whom it serves and what it offers does it work leftward to activities, resources and partnerships, and downward to cost and revenue.

    Two mistakes to watch for: a generous value proposition paired with a cost structure that cannot support it; a customer segment with no channel to reach it. Both show up at once on the single page.

    Primer 2.13 · Adapting the model to India

    Four features of the Indian market shape adaptation:

    • Price sensitivity is high — customers weigh cost carefully and resist a premium.
    • Regional diversity is wide — many languages, tastes and income levels.
    • Infrastructure gaps remain in logistics and connectivity.
    • Regulatory complexity adds a further layer.
    Ola's India-first adaptationsWhere the global ride-hailing model assumed card payments and private cars, Ola accepted cash (most Indian customers did not use cards for small daily payments), integrated auto-rickshaws (the vehicles many riders actually use), and offered apps in local languages. These changes were not cosmetic; they were what allowed a familiar model to succeed in an unfamiliar market.

    Distribution is often the hardest part to adapt. In a country where many customers live far from organised retail and trust is built locally, reaching them can cost more than serving them. Some ventures solve this by working through existing local networks — neighbourhood shops, community agents — rather than building a distribution system from nothing.

    Common challenges in business-model development (from slides): misjudging customer demand; copy-pasting Western models to the Indian context; balancing scale vs profitability; regulatory hurdles.

    Primer 2.14 · What a value proposition is

    A value proposition states, in plain terms, why a customer should choose one product or service over the alternatives. A strong one answers three questions clearly: what problem are we solving? For whom? And why is our solution better than the options the customer already has?

    The most common mistake is to list features rather than benefits. A customer does not care that a product has a particular specification; the customer cares what the product does for them. The discipline of translating every feature into a benefit — and then testing whether that benefit matters to the customer — is what turns a description into a proposition.

    Primer 2.15 · The Value Proposition Canvas

    The VPC, a companion to the BMC (Osterwalder et al., Value Proposition Design, 2014), has two parts:

    Customer Profile (circle)Value Map (square)
    Customer jobs — functional, social and emotional tasks the customer is trying to completeProducts & services — what the firm provides
    Pains — obstacles, frustrations, risksPain relievers — how the offering eases pains
    Gains — outcomes and benefits hoped forGain creators — how it produces the gains the customer wants

    Fit is achieved when pain relievers and gain creators match the most important pains and gains. Building the two sides separately, then comparing them, tends to expose comfortable assumptions that would otherwise go unquestioned.

    Customer jobs repay a closer look. A job may be functional (getting to work on time), social (being seen as reliable), or emotional (feeling secure). A founder who attends only to the functional job often misses the reason a customer really chooses one option over another. A ride-hailing service, for example, sells not only transport but also the assurance of a fair price and a safe driver — social and emotional gains as much as functional ones.

    Primer 2.16 · Achieving problem-solution fit

    Three signs mark this point:

    1. Customers recognise the problem clearly, without being persuaded that they have it.
    2. The solution addresses the most critical pain, not a minor one.
    3. Customers show a willingness to pay for the solution or to switch from what they use now.

    The cost of scaling before this fit is reached is high. A firm that spends heavily on marketing and hiring while its product still fails to solve a real problem simply burns money faster. Fit is not a guarantee of success, but its absence is a reliable predictor of failure.

    Primer 2.17 · Revenue models

    A revenue model describes how a startup earns money. It sits inside the business model but is distinct from the product and from the business model as a whole. In India the choice is shaped by particular conditions:

    • High price sensitivity — customers resist paying directly for what they can get free.
    • Large informal economy — many transactions happen in cash and outside formal systems.
    • Low switching costs — customers move easily from one service to another.

    Challenges and unit economics

    Choosing and running a revenue model in India brings recurring challenges:

    • Growth vs profitability tension — capturing a price-sensitive market often means keeping prices low, which delays profit.
    • Copying Western models without adapting — frequently fails because Indian customers behave differently.
    • Regulatory compliance — adds cost and constrains how revenue is earned.
    • Sustainability of unit economics — each transaction must eventually contribute more than it costs.

    Two figures capture the health of a revenue model. CAC (customer acquisition cost) and LTV/CLV (lifetime value) must stand in a sustainable ratio. If it costs more to win a customer than the customer will ever be worth, the business loses money on every sale, and growth only deepens the loss. A healthy venture keeps CAC well below LTV, and watches the gap as it grows — a model that is sound at small scale can turn unsound when competition raises the cost of winning each new customer. The rule of thumb: LTV : CAC ≥ 3 : 1.

    Primer 2.18 · Social entrepreneurship in India

    Social entrepreneurship combines profitability with social impact. A social enterprise sets out to solve a societal problem — poverty, poor health, lack of energy — while remaining financially self-sustaining rather than depending on donations. This dual aim distinguishes it from:

    • A pure charity (impact without a profit motive).
    • A conventional startup (profit without a social mission).

    The two goals need not conflict. In many cases the social mission opens a market that others have ignored, and serving that market well is what generates the revenue.

    The entrepreneurship spectrum: non-profit (impact-first) → social enterprise → hybrid → for-profit startup (profit-first).

    Key thinkers: John Elkington (1994) coined the triple bottom line — People, Planet, Profit. Bill Drayton (founder of Ashoka, 1980) popularised "social entrepreneur". Muhammad Yunus (Grameen Bank, Nobel Peace Prize 2006) developed the "social business" idea.

    The dual-mission difficulty: a social enterprise must measure two kinds of success, financial and social, and the second is far harder to quantify. Revenue and profit are easily counted; impact — whether measured in health, education or livelihoods — resists a single number. Ventures that take their mission seriously invest in honest measurement of impact and resist the temptation to claim more than they can show. Where the two missions pull in different directions, the founder must decide which takes priority, and an answer settled in advance prevents drift.

    Why India is fertile ground: large numbers of people remain underserved in energy, health, finance and education, which means solving a social problem well can also open a substantial market. The same conditions that make these problems pressing — low incomes and thin infrastructure — also demand solutions that are affordable and simple. Social entrepreneurship in India is therefore closely tied to frugal innovation, and the two often appear together in a single venture.

    Primer 2.19 · Frugal innovation

    Frugal innovation, often called jugaad in the Indian context, is the practice of creating useful solutions under tight constraints. It rests on three principles:

    1. Affordability — reaches customers with limited means.
    2. Simplicity — strips away features that add cost without adding value.
    3. Scalability — can spread widely at low additional cost.

    Together these produce products that serve large numbers of price-sensitive customers — a task at which conventional innovation, aimed at wealthier buyers, often fails.

    Examples across Indian industryThe Jaipur Foot delivers a functional prosthetic limb at a fraction of the cost of imported alternatives. The Tata Nano attempted to bring car ownership within reach of families who had only owned two-wheelers. Low-cost diagnostic devices have extended basic healthcare to places where expensive equipment could never go. Agnibaan, a small satellite launch vehicle developed by Agnikul Cosmos (a startup incubated at IIT Madras), is a recent frugal example. ISRO's Mangalyaan (Mars Orbiter Mission), completed for roughly ₹450 crore, is often cited as frugal engineering at its best.

    Frugal innovation is not mere cost-cutting. Removing quality to lower a price produces a worse product, not a frugal one. True frugal innovation rethinks a product so that it delivers the outcome the customer needs using far fewer resources — a harder and more creative task.

    Reverse innovation is the related idea in which a product designed for a poor market is later adopted in a rich one, because affordability and simplicity turn out to have value everywhere. In this way the constraints of the Indian market have become a training ground for ideas with worldwide reach.

    The link between the two themes: a social enterprise sets out to serve people whom conventional business has overlooked, and those people are usually price-sensitive and hard to reach. Frugal innovation supplies the method by which such customers can be served at a profit. Together they describe a distinctively Indian style of entrepreneurship.

    1. Must always be patented
    2. Involves the successful commercial or social application of an idea
    3. Is always a physical product
    4. Happens only in R&D laboratories
    Answer (b). Invention creates something new; innovation makes it useful and available. Patents are optional (many business-model innovations are not patented). Innovation can be a service, process or model, and it also happens in the field, in firms and through users.

    4. Attention → Interest → Desire → Action
    Answer (c). Build–Measure–Learn turns ideas into products, products into data, and data into learning. PDCA (a) is Deming's quality cycle, (b) is design thinking, and AIDA (d) is an advertising model.

    An MVP is best defined as:

    1. The version of a product that allows a team to collect the maximum validated learning with the least effort
    2. The cheapest possible final product
    3. A product with all features but lower quality
    4. A beta version released only to investors
    Answer (a). This is Ries's definition: the MVP is a learning tool. It is not a cheap final product (b), nor a full feature set at low quality (c). It is aimed at real customers, not investors (d).

    A "concierge MVP" means:

    1. Customer service delivered by AI
    2. The service is delivered manually, by hand, to early customers so the team learns directly
    3. A landing page that measures sign-ups
    4. A rough working prototype
    Answer (b). In a concierge MVP the founder openly performs the service by hand for a few customers. A Wizard-of-Oz MVP looks automated while humans work behind the scenes. A landing-page MVP gauges interest before building. A prototype MVP is a rough working model.

    Swiggy's early MVP was:

    1. An AI-powered app
    2. A simple website with about 20 restaurants, orders coordinated over the phone, and deliveries arranged by hand
    3. A drone-based delivery service
    4. A franchise of physical outlets
    Answer (b). The bare version tested whether customers wanted food delivered and whether the logistics could work — without the cost of building elaborate technology first. Only after demand was proven did the company invest in scaling systems.

    Paytm's shift from mobile recharge to a broad payments and financial-services platform is a well-known example of:

    1. Failure
    2. A pivot
    3. A layoff
    4. An acquisition
    Answer (b). A pivot keeps what was learned and changes a fundamental element of the strategy (market, product, revenue logic). Paytm's shift opened a far larger opportunity than the original recharge business.

    Which is an actionable rather than a vanity metric?

    1. Total cumulative app downloads
    2. Total page views since launch
    3. Week-4 retention of users who signed up in a given cohort
    4. Number of social-media followers
    Answer (c). Cohort retention shows cause and effect and guides decisions. Cumulative downloads, page views and followers only go up and feel good, but they do not show whether the product creates lasting value.

    The Business Model Canvas was developed by:

    1. Steve Blank and Eric Ries
    2. Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur
    3. Michael Porter
    4. Clayton Christensen
    Answer (b). They introduced it in Business Model Generation (2010). Ries and Blank are lean startup and customer development. Porter is Five Forces and value chain. Christensen is disruption.

    How many building blocks does the Business Model Canvas have?

    1. 5
    2. 7
    3. 9
    4. 12
    Answer (c). Nine: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. 5 may be confused with Porter's forces, 7 with the 7Ps of services marketing.

    In the Business Model Canvas, "Customer Relationships" deals primarily with:

    1. How customers perceive your value
    2. How you price your products
    3. How you partner with distributors
    4. How you acquire, retain and grow customers
    Answer (d). The block describes the relationship each segment expects — self-service, personal support, community, loyalty programmes such as Swiggy One. Pricing is revenue streams; distributors belong to channels; perception is value propositions.

    In the Value Proposition Canvas, the customer profile consists of:

    1. Products, services, features
    2. Customer jobs, pains, gains
    3. Pain relievers, gain creators, channels
    4. Revenue, cost, profit
    Answer (b). The circle is customer jobs, pains and gains. The square (value map) holds products and services, pain relievers and gain creators. Channels belongs to the BMC; revenue/cost/profit is a financial summary.

    "Fit" in the Value Proposition Canvas is achieved when:

    1. Pain relievers and gain creators address the most important pains and gains of the customer profile
    2. The product has more features than competitors
    3. Revenue exceeds cost
    4. All nine BMC blocks are filled
    Answer (a). Fit means the value map matches what customers really care about. More features (b) can add clutter without relieving pains. Profitability (c) and a filled canvas (d) are different milestones.

    Ola's India-first adaptations in the ride-hailing market included:

    1. Card-only payments and luxury cars
    2. Cash payments, auto-rickshaws, and regional-language apps
    3. Refusing to serve tier-2 cities
    4. Copying Uber's global model exactly
    Answer (b). Where the global model assumed card payments and private cars, Ola accepted cash (most Indian customers did not use cards for small daily payments), integrated auto-rickshaws, and offered local-language apps. These adaptations — not the underlying app — are what allowed the model to succeed in India.

    A music app offers ad-supported free listening and an ad-free paid tier with downloads. This revenue model is:

    1. Marketplace
    2. Freemium
    3. Direct sales
    4. Licensing
    Answer (b). A free base tier plus a premium upgrade is freemium (Byju's, Hotstar, JioSaavn). A marketplace earns commission between two sides; direct sales sells your own goods; licensing charges others for rights to use IP.

    The main revenue source of a marketplace such as Ola, Urban Company or Flipkart is:

    1. Manufacturing margins on its own products
    2. Commission (a "take rate") on transactions between buyers and sellers, plus listing and service fees and advertising
    3. Government grants
    4. Interest income from customer deposits
    Answer (b). Marketplaces monetise the matching of two sides. Manufacturing margin (a) belongs to D2C or brands. Grants (c) are not a business model. Deposits (d) are a banking model.

    boAt, Mamaearth and Lenskart selling their own branded products through their own websites and stores is an example of:

    1. Freemium
    2. Direct sales / direct-to-consumer (D2C)
    3. Marketplace
    4. Advertisement-based
    Answer (b). D2C brands sell their own products directly and keep the full margin and the customer data. They may also list on marketplaces, but their defining model is direct sales.

    A healthy startup is often expected to keep its LTV (lifetime value) relative to its CAC (customer acquisition cost) at about:

    1. 0.5 : 1
    2. 1 : 1
    3. 3 : 1 or higher
    4. It does not matter
    Answer (c). A lifetime value of at least three times the acquisition cost suggests sustainable unit economics. At 0.5:1 you lose money on every customer; at 1:1 you only break even before overheads. Unit economics definitely matter to investors.

    SELCO India, founded by Harish Hande, is best known for:

    1. Microfinance for urban traders
    2. Solar energy solutions with doorstep financing for the rural poor
    3. Mid-day meals at scale
    4. Organic skincare for millennials
    Answer (b). SELCO combines solar lighting with tailored financing. Mid-day meals is Akshaya Patra. Microfinance is associated with SKS or Bandhan, not SELCO. Organic skincare is a distractor.

    Frugal innovation rests on three principles:

    1. Speed, cost, quality
    2. Affordability, simplicity, scalability
    3. Design, patent, market
    4. Premium, exclusivity, luxury
    Answer (b). Frugal innovation strips a product to its essential value at low cost (affordability), removes non-essential features (simplicity), and can spread widely at low marginal cost (scalability). The Jaipur Foot (~$50 vs ~$8,000) is the classic example. It is not mere cost-cutting — quality is preserved.
