    Unit Zero · Before We Begin

    How a Computer Works, from Zero

    The handful of ideas that the whole subject is built on, explained with a kitchen.

    Not a syllabus unit by itself, but every word here (CPU, cache, RAM, bit, byte, clock, cycle, address, transistor, die) appears in Units 1 and 2 and in the numericals. Read it once slowly. It takes about 25 minutes.

    Step 0.1The computer as a kitchen

    The big ideaA computer has a worker (the processor) and places to keep things (memory). Places close to the worker are fast but small. Places far away are big but slow. Most of this subject is about that one trade-off.

    Picture a chef cooking in a restaurant kitchen:

    In the kitchenIn the computerSizeHow fast to reach
    The chef's handsRegisters, inside the CPUA few itemsInstantly
    The counter right next to the chefCache (L1, L2, L3), on the CPU chipSmall (KB to MB)Very fast
    The fridge across the roomMain memory (RAM)Medium (GB)Slow-ish (a walk)
    The supermarket down the roadStorage (SSD, hard disk)Huge (TB)Very slow (a drive)

    The chef is the processor, also called the CPU (Central Processing Unit). It follows a recipe (the program) one step at a time. A good kitchen keeps whatever the chef needs right now on the counter, so the chef rarely has to walk to the fridge. That is exactly what a cache does (Unit 2).

    ExampleReading from the counter (cache) takes about 1 nanosecond. Walking to the fridge (RAM) takes about 100 nanoseconds. If the chef had to walk to the fridge for every ingredient, cooking would be 100 times slower. So the counter matters enormously, even though it is tiny.

    Register

    Cache

    Storage

    Step 0.2Bits, bytes and powers of two (you need this for every cache numerical)

    The big ideaEverything inside a computer is stored as 0s and 1s. With n bits you can write 2ⁿ different numbers. Turning a size into "how many bits do I need?" is just asking "2 to the power of what?", which is called log₂.

    A bit is one 0-or-1 switch. 8 bits = 1 byte. A word is the chunk the CPU normally handles at once (32 or 64 bits, depending on the machine; each question tells you).

    Why powers of two?

    1 bit can say 2 things (0, 1). 2 bits can say 4 things (00, 01, 10, 11). 3 bits can say 8 things. Every extra bit doubles the count. So n bits → 2ⁿ possibilities.

    PowerValueName used in questions
    2⁵32
    2⁸256
    2¹⁰1,0241 K (kilo)
    2²⁰1,048,5761 M (mega)
    2³⁰about a billion1 G (giga)

    So 16 KB = 16 × 2¹⁰ = 2⁴ × 2¹⁰ = 2¹⁴ bytes. Break the number into "a power of two × K/M/G", then add the powers.

    What is log₂?

    log₂(x) answers "2 to the power of what gives x?". log₂(8) = 3 because 2³ = 8. log₂(256) = 8. log₂(1024) = 10.

    Example"A block is 32 bytes. How many bits do I need to point at one byte inside the block?" There are 32 positions, and 32 = 2⁵, so you need 5 bits. This single trick is most of every cache numerical in § 2.3.

    Step 0.3Addresses: every byte has a house number

    The big ideaMemory is like a very long street of houses. Each house (each byte) has a number called its address. If addresses are n bits long, the street can have 2ⁿ houses.

    When the CPU wants a piece of data, it sends out the address, and memory sends back what is stored at that house.

    ExampleMain memory is 128 KB = 2¹⁷ bytes. To give every byte its own number you need 17-bit addresses. Going the other way, a 32-bit address can reach 2³² bytes = 4 GB.

    Watch out for the "addressable unit". Usually each address points to one byte. Sometimes a question says memory is addressed by word or half-word. Then each house holds that many bytes, so you count in words or half-words instead of bytes. § 2.3 shows this trap.

    New words
    Address
    The number that identifies one location in memory
    Addressable unit
    How much data one address points to (usually a byte)
    Address space
    All the addresses a program is allowed to use

    Step 0.4Instructions and the clock

    The big ideaA program is a list of tiny instructions ("add these two numbers", "load this from memory"). The CPU works to the beat of a clock. Each beat is one cycle. Some instructions take one cycle, some take several.

    The clock is like a metronome. Clock rate (frequency, f) is how many beats per second, measured in hertz. Cycle time (T) is the length of one beat. They are opposites of each other:

    Cycle time = 1 / Clock rate 1 GHz = 10⁹ beats per second → one cycle = 1 nanosecond (ns) 2 GHz → one cycle = 0.5 ns

    How many beats an instruction takes on average is called CPI (Cycles Per Instruction). That one number is the heart of the "Iron Law" in § 1.9:

    ExampleA program runs 1,000 instructions. Each takes 2 cycles on average (CPI = 2). The clock is 1 GHz (1 ns per cycle). Time = 1,000 × 2 × 1 ns = 2,000 ns = 2 µs.

    Step 0.5Transistors, chips, wafers and dies

    The big ideaA processor is made of billions of microscopic on/off switches called transistors. Chips are printed many at a time on a round disc of silicon (a wafer), then cut apart. Each little square is a die. Some dies come out broken, and that is what the "yield" numericals in § 1.7 are about.

    Think of baking square biscuits on a round tray. The biscuits at the round edge get cut off and wasted. If a speck of dust lands on a biscuit, that biscuit is ruined. Bigger biscuits mean fewer fit on the tray, and each one is more likely to catch a speck. So big chips are much more expensive.

    New words
    Transistor
    A microscopic electronic switch; the basic building block of every chip
    Wafer
    A thin round disc of silicon on which many chips are made at once
    Die
    One chip-sized square cut from the wafer
    Yield
    The fraction of dies that work (e.g. 0.66 = 66% are good)

    Step 0.6Doing many things at once: parallelism

    The big ideaYou can make work finish faster by using more workers at the same time. That is called parallelism. But some steps can only be done by one worker, and those steps limit how much faster you can get (Amdahl's law, § 1.11).

    Modern chips have several cores: several chefs in the same kitchen. Four cores can chop four onions at once. But if the recipe says "wait for the oven", adding chefs doesn't help that part.

    ExampleA job takes 10 minutes: 2 minutes of "only one person can do this" and 8 minutes that can be shared. With 4 people: 2 + 8/4 = 4 minutes. With 1,000 people: 2 + 0.008 ≈ 2 minutes. You can never go below 2 minutes, however many people you add.
    New words
    Core
    One complete processor on a chip; a multicore chip has several
    Parallelism
    Doing several pieces of work at the same moment
    Speedup
    Old time ÷ new time (e.g. 10 min → 4 min is a speedup of 2.5)

    § 1.1What Is Computer Architecture? What Is an ISA?

    The big ideaComputer architecture is the design plan of a computer: how its hardware and software fit together. The most important part of that plan is the ISA (Instruction Set Architecture), the list of instructions the processor understands. The ISA is a promise: software is written for it, and hardware is built to keep it.

    What does "architecture" mean for a computer?

    An architect takes bricks, steel and glass and designs a building that suits its purpose. A computer architect takes transistors, memory and wires and designs a machine that suits its purpose: fast enough, small enough and cheap enough.

    The professor's definition has three parts:

    1. System design: the hardware parts and how they connect: CPU, memory controllers, multiprocessors, DMA.
    2. Instruction Set Architecture (ISA): the processor's "language": its instructions, data formats and registers, as seen by programmers.
    3. Microarchitecture: the actual circuits inside the CPU (data paths, storage) that carry out the ISA.

    Why is the ISA in the middle of everything?

    A computer is built in layers, like a cake. Each layer only needs to know about the one directly below it. The ISA is the layer where software stops and hardware starts. Programs and compilers above it only see the ISA. The circuits below it only exist to carry out the ISA.

    The professor sums up the designer's job as A = F / M: Architecture is meeting the Functional needs within the limits of the available Materials (technology).

    Architecture vs organisation: what's the difference?

    ExampleTwo cars can both have "a steering wheel, an accelerator and a brake" (what the driver sees). Under the bonnet, one has a petrol engine and one has an electric motor.
    What the driver sees = architecture. What's under the bonnet = organisation.
    Computer architectureComputer organisation
    What the programmer can see: instructions, data types, addressing modes, number of registersThe hidden hardware that makes it work: control signals, interfaces, memory technology
    Answers what the machine doesAnswers how it does it
    "Is there a multiply instruction?""Is multiply done by a special circuit or by repeated adding?"
    Stays the same for decades (x86 since 1978)Changes every generation (8086 → Core i9)

    What exactly does an ISA list?

    The ISA is everything a programmer can see and use. It is enough information to write a program in machine language:

    • Memory organisation: how many addresses exist (address space) and how many bits live at each address (addressability).
    • Registers: how many, how big, what each is for.
    • Instructions: the operation codes (opcodes), the data types (byte, word, float), and the addressing modes, meaning the ways an instruction can say where its data is (immediate, register, direct, indirect, displacement, indexed).

    A good ISA is: implementable (can be built cheaply or expensively), programmable (easy for compilers to use) and compatible (old programs keep working on new chips: 8086 → 286 → 386 → 486 → Pentium all run the same programs).

    How does the CPU run one instruction? The five stages (asked in 2022)

    Every instruction goes through the same five steps, like a car on an assembly line:

    1. IF, Instruction Fetch: get the next instruction from memory. The address is in the Program Counter (PC). Then move the PC on: PC ← PC + 4.
    2. ID, Instruction Decode: work out what the instruction means, set up the control signals, and read the registers it needs.
    3. EX, Execute: the ALU (the calculator inside the CPU) does the maths, or works out a memory address, or checks a branch condition.
    4. MEM, Memory access: a load reads from memory; a store writes to it. Other instructions skip this step.
    5. WB, Write Back: put the result into the destination register.
    ExampleLW R1, 8(R2) means "load into R1 the value at address R2 + 8".
    IF fetches it → ID reads R2 → EX calculates R2 + 8 → MEM reads that memory location → WB writes the value into R1.
    ADD R3, R1, R4 does nothing in the MEM stage, because it doesn't touch memory.

    Extensions add special instructions: Intel MMX, SSE, SSE2, AVX and AMD 3DNow! all do the same operation on many numbers at once (SIMD, § 1.4). RISC-V is a newer ISA that is free for anyone to use.

    Why does the ISA matter so much in real life?

    When Apple moved Macs from Intel (x86) to its own M1 chip (ARM) in 2020, the ISA changed, so every app had to be rebuilt or translated (by Rosetta 2). When Intel went from Pentium 4 to Core, only the organisation changed, so nothing needed rebuilding. Once software depends on an ISA, it stays for decades.

    CISC vs RISC. CISC (x86) uses powerful, complicated instructions, so programs need fewer of them, but each is harder to run fast. RISC (ARM, MIPS, RISC-V) uses simple instructions that are easy to pipeline and clock fast, but programs need more of them. Modern x86 chips secretly break their CISC instructions into simple RISC-like steps ("micro-ops") inside.

    "Myopic" means short-sighted. The myopic view says computer architecture is only about designing the instruction set.     § 1.3Moore's Law and What It Means

    The big ideaThe number of transistors on an affordable chip doubles about every two years. It is not a law of nature, just an observation that the industry turned into a target. It held for about 50 years and made computers billions of times more powerful.

    Who said it, and what exactly?

    Gordon Moore, co-founder of Intel, in Electronics magazine on 19 April 1965. He noticed that the number of transistors that could be cheaply put on a chip doubled roughly every 18–24 months. It is a rule of thumb, not a physical law.

    How do I calculate with it?

    Pn = P0 × 2^n P0 = transistor count (or processing power) in the starting year Pn = the count n doubling-periods later n = (number of years) / 2 ← one doubling every two years
    Example (the professor's)In 1988 the Intel 386 SX had 275,000 transistors. Predict the Pentium II in 1997.
    Years = 1997 − 1988 = 9, so n = 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 doublings.
    Pn = 275,000 × 2^4.5 = 275,000 × 22.63 ≈ 6.2 million transistors.
    The real Pentium II had about 7.5 million, so the rule was close.

    What did it make possible?

    • Computing got cheap. A chip with 2,000 transistors cost $1,000 in 1970, $500 in 1972, $250 in 1974, $0.97 in 1990, and under $0.02 today. A $3,000 PC from 1990 would cost about $5 to build today.
    • Everything linked to chips improved too: speed, memory size, camera sensors.
    • New products every couple of years: twice the capability means new things become possible, new competitors appear, and old companies die.
    • For architects: a growing "transistor budget" to spend on caches, pipelines and extra cores.

    Why is it slowing down, and what did the industry do? (asked in 2023)

    Moore himself said it can't continue forever: transistors are now only a few dozen atoms wide. Physicist Michio Kaku predicted a collapse "in about ten years or so", and Intel has admitted the slowdown. Three walls hit at the same time:

    1. Power wall: around 2005, shrinking transistors stopped reducing their power (the end of "Dennard scaling"). Faster clocks meant melting chips, so clock speeds stopped at about 3–5 GHz.
    2. ILP wall: squeezing more parallel work out of a single stream of instructions gave smaller and smaller gains.
    3. Memory wall: memory got faster far more slowly than processors, so processors spent more time waiting.

    How the industry adapted:

    1. Multi-core processors: instead of one faster core, put 2, 4 … 96 cores on one chip (Intel Core Duo 2006, AMD EPYC today). Software now has to split its work (and Amdahl's law, § 1.11, limits the gain).
    2. Better memory hierarchy: bigger, multi-level caches (L1/L2/L3, AMD 3D V-Cache), HBM memory on the package, prefetching (Unit 2).
    3. Specialised chips: GPUs, TPUs and NPUs that are 10–100× more efficient at one kind of job.
    4. 3D stacking and chiplets: stacking layers of chips, or building one processor from several small chips (AMD Ryzen, Apple M-Ultra).
    5. New transistor designs and materials: FinFET → gate-all-around, EUV lithography; further out, quantum computing.
    6. Power management: lowering voltage and speed when idle (DVFS), turbo boost, switching off unused parts ("dark silicon").

    So is Moore's law dead?

    Only half. Transistor counts are still rising (through 3D stacking and density), but the cost per transistor has stopped falling at the newest nodes, and single-core speed now improves by under 5% a year instead of about 50%. The "free speed-up" for software is over. It was also partly a self-fulfilling prophecy: companies planned their roadmaps around it.

    § 1.4Types of Parallel Computers: Flynn's Taxonomy

    The big ideaAsk two questions about any computer: how many instruction streams run at once, and how many data streams? Each answer is "single" or "multiple", so there are four boxes: SISD, SIMD, MISD, MIMD. Michael Flynn proposed this in 1966.

    What do "instruction stream" and "data stream" mean?

    An instruction stream is a sequence of commands ("add, then multiply, then store"). A data stream is the sequence of numbers those commands work on.

    Example: a school exam hall SISD: one teacher marks one answer sheet at a time.
    SIMD: the head teacher shouts "add 5 marks to question 3" and 40 teachers do it to 40 different sheets at the same moment.
    MISD: three teachers each check the same sheet in different ways, and they vote if they disagree.
    MIMD: 40 teachers, each marking a different subject on a different sheet, independently.

    The four classes, properly

    1. SISD (Single Instruction, Single Data): the ordinary one-processor computer. One instruction works on one piece of data at a time, in order (the "von Neumann" design). It can still use pipelining. Examples: early PCs, simple microcontrollers.
    2. SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data): the same instruction runs on many pieces of data at once (data parallelism). Examples: vector processors, array processors, SSE/AVX/ARM NEON instructions, GPUs. Great for images, matrices and machine learning.
    3. MISD (Multiple Instruction, Single Data): different instructions on the same data. Rare. Examples: fault-tolerant computers that run the same calculation several ways and vote (Space Shuttle flight computers), systolic arrays.
    4. MIMD (Multiple Instruction, Multiple Data): each processor runs its own instructions on its own data. The most flexible and the most common. Examples: multicore CPUs, servers, clusters, supercomputers.

    What role did Flynn's classification play in history? (asked in 2022)

    It gave everyone a shared vocabulary for parallel computers, and each class matches a period of history:

    • 1940s–70s, SISD era: von Neumann machines, sped up by faster technology and then pipelining.
    • 1970s–80s, SIMD rises: ILLIAC IV, the Cray-1 vector supercomputer (1976), the Connection Machine, built for scientific work on huge arrays.
    • 1990s, MIMD takes over: ordinary microprocessors became so cheap that clusters of them (Beowulf, IBM SP2) beat custom vector machines.
    • 1990s–2000s, SIMD moves inside: MMX, SSE and AVX put SIMD into every core.
    • 2005 to today, mixtures: multicore CPUs (MIMD) plus GPUs (SIMD) plus accelerators. A modern laptop contains all four classes.

    Its weaknesses: it is too coarse (almost everything today is "MIMD", and the label says nothing about memory or programming); the MISD box is nearly empty; it can't describe hybrids like GPUs ("SIMT", single instruction multiple threads); it ignores whether the parallel pieces are small or large. Later schemes (Feng, Händler, Duncan) extend it, but Flynn is still the starting point in every textbook.

    § 1.5Technology Trends: Bandwidth, Latency, Transistors & Wires

    The big ideaA chip designed today ships in 3–5 years, so architects must design for future technology. Two lessons from the trends: bandwidth improves much faster than latency, and wires don't get faster the way transistors do.

    Which technologies shape computer design, and how fast do they improve?

    The professor lists four families of trends: technology, power, cost and reliability. The technology trends are:

    TechnologyRoughly how fast it improves each year
    Logic chipsTransistor density ~35% · die size ~10–20% · transistors per chip ~40–55%
    Memory (DRAM)Capacity ~40% historically (now slower: doubling every 3–4 years or more)
    Storage (disk)Density ~30% since 2004; flash (SSD) capacity 50–60%
    NetworkLink speeds 1 → 10 → 100 → 400 Gb/s Ethernet

    Bandwidth vs latency: what's the difference?

    Example: a motorwayLatency is how long one car takes to drive from A to B. Bandwidth is how many cars reach B per hour. Adding more lanes raises bandwidth a lot, but no car arrives any sooner.
    ComponentBandwidth got better byLatency got better by
    Processors10,000 – 25,000×only 30 – 80×
    Memory & disks300 – 1,200×only 6 – 8×

    Rule of thumb: bandwidth improves at least as fast as the square of the latency improvement. Why? You can buy bandwidth by doing more in parallel (wider buses, more memory banks, more cores). Latency is stuck with physics: distance and the speed of signals. So designers hide latency (caches, prefetching, pipelining) rather than remove it.

    Why don't wires keep up with transistors?

    When chips shrink, transistors get smaller and faster. Wires get thinner and closer together too, which increases their resistance and capacitance per millimetre. Wire delay ∝ Resistance × Capacitance, so it doesn't improve. More of each clock cycle is spent just moving signals along wires. The fix is to split designs into small local regions, which is another push towards multicore.

    What technology advances made processors so fast? (asked in 2023)

    1. Smaller transistors (Moore's law): more and faster transistors every generation (10 µm in 1971 → about 3 nm today).
    2. Pipelining: overlapping instructions like an assembly line, so the clock can tick faster.
    3. Superscalar and out-of-order execution: several instructions per cycle, so CPI drops below 1.
    4. Branch prediction and speculative execution: guess which way a branch goes so the pipeline stays full.
    5. Cache hierarchies: L1/L2/L3 hide slow memory (Unit 2).
    6. RISC design and smart compilers: simple instructions that are easy to pipeline.
    7. SIMD/vector extensions: MMX → SSE → AVX-512, doing many numbers per instruction.
    8. Multicore and multithreading (Hyper-Threading): after the power wall.
    9. Power management: voltage dropped from 5 V to under 1 V; DVFS and turbo modes.
    10. Specialised accelerators: GPUs, TPUs, NPUs.

    Link each to the Iron Law (§ 1.9) for extra marks: smaller transistors and pipelining cut the clock time; superscalar, caches and prediction cut the CPI; better ISAs, compilers and SIMD cut the instruction count.

    Critical point: each trick eventually hits diminishing returns. Deeper pipelines waste more on wrong guesses; wider superscalar finds little extra parallel work; caches can't fix bad locality. That's why the yearly improvement dropped from about 52% to about 23% (and lower) around 2005.

    § 1.6Power and Energy

    The big ideaEvery time a transistor flips, it uses a little energy, and the cost goes with the voltage squared. So lowering the voltage is the best way to save energy. Lowering only the clock speed saves power but not energy, because the job just takes longer.

    Power vs energy: which is which?

    Power is how fast energy is used right now (watts). It decides how hot the chip gets and how big the cooling must be. Energy is the total used for a whole task (joules): Energy = Power × Time. It decides battery life and electricity bills. Energy is the true cost of doing a task; power is a limit you must stay under.

    The formulas

    Total Power = Dynamic Power + Static Power Power_dynamic = ½ × Capacitive load × Voltage² × Frequency switched Energy_dynamic = Capacitive load × Voltage² (per 0→1→0 flip; ½CV² per single flip) Power_static = I_static × V ("leakage") Energy = Power × Time
    • Dynamic power is used when transistors switch. Each switch charges or empties a tiny capacitor.
    • Static power (leakage) is used even when nothing switches, because tiny transistors leak current.

    Clock ↓ → power ↓ but energy unchanged (runs slower, same total). Voltage ↓ → power ↓ and energy ↓. That's why chip voltages fell from 5 V to under 1 V in 20 years.

    What are peak power and TDP?

    • Getting power in and heat out: the chip needs hundreds of pins to bring power in, and the heat has to be removed.
    • Peak power: the power supply must handle the maximum draw. If the chip pulls more current than the supply can give, the voltage sags ("voltage droop") and the chip malfunctions.
    • TDP (Thermal Design Power): the sustained power the cooling system is designed for. Lower than peak, higher than average.
    • Energy efficiency: considers power and time together (energy per task).

    Worked examples (the professor's)

    Example 1: which processor uses less energy?Processor A uses 20% more power than B but finishes in 30% less time.
    Energy of A relative to B = 1.2 (power) × 0.7 (time) = 0.84.
    So A uses 16% less energy and is better, as long as the power supply and cooling can handle its higher power.
    Example 2: cut voltage and frequency by 15%Capacitance stays the same. Dynamic power ∝ V² × f.
    New ÷ old = (0.85)² × 0.85 = 0.85³ = 0.614.
    Dynamic power falls to about 61% (≈ 60%) of the original.
    Example 3: two modesMode 1: 1 V, 3 GHz. Mode 2: 0.75 V, 2 GHz.
    Energy ∝ V²: ratio = (0.75)² ÷ (1)² = 0.5625, so ≈ 44% energy saved.
    Power ∝ V² × f: ratio = (0.75² × 2) ÷ (1² × 3) = 0.375, so 62.5% power saved.

    Why is static power a growing problem?

    P_static = I_static × V. Smaller transistors leak more, and leakage rises exponentially with temperature and as the threshold voltage (VTh) is lowered to keep chips fast at low voltage. Leakage can reach 25–50% of total power. Fixes: power gating (cut power to idle blocks), multiple threshold voltages, and "race to halt" (finish fast, then sleep).

    A real story: the power wall

    Intel planned for its Pentium 4 to reach 10 GHz. The Prescott version (2004) already used about 100 W at 3.8 GHz and the heat couldn't be removed cheaply, so the next chip (Tejas) was cancelled. Intel switched to the slower-clocked, multicore Core design. Since Dennard scaling ended, chips have "dark silicon": they can't power all their transistors at once. Today's answers: DVFS (Intel SpeedStep, ARM big.LITTLE), turbo boost, clock gating and power gating. Voltage can't drop much below about 0.7 V before transistors become slow and unreliable, so performance-per-watt is now the main design goal.

    § 1.7Cost of a Chip and Fabrication Yield

    The big ideaChips are made on a round wafer and cut into dies. Some dies are ruined by tiny defects. A bigger die means fewer dies per wafer and a higher chance each one is ruined. The two effects multiply, so die cost rises very steeply with size (roughly with area⁴).

    Cost vs price

    Cost is what it takes the company to make the product (materials, manufacturing). Price is what the customer pays. Profit = Price − Cost: sell for $10 something that cost $6, and you earn $4.

    Three things push cost down:

    • Time (the learning curve): factories get better at making a chip over time, so yield rises and cost falls.
    • Volume: making more units spreads the design cost and improves efficiency.
    • Commodification: when many companies sell nearly identical products (DRAM, PCs), competition pushes cost close to price.

    How is a chip made?

    A silicon crystal (ingot) is sliced into wafers → the wafers go through many steps (patterning with light, doping, etching, adding metal layers) → wafers are tested → good wafers are cut into dies → dies are tested → good dies are packaged, tested again, and shipped. Example: Intel Sandy Bridge got 280 dies from one 300 mm wafer at 32 nm.

    The formulas (the professor's)

    Cost of die + Cost of testing die + Cost of packaging & final test Cost of integrated circuit = ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── Final test yield Cost of wafer Cost of die = ─────────────────────────── Dies per wafer × Die yield π × (Wafer diameter / 2)² π × Wafer diameter Dies per wafer = ─────────────────────────── − ────────────────────── Die area √(2 × Die area) (wafer area ÷ die area) (part-dies wasted at the round edge) Bose-Einstein formula: Die yield = Wafer yield × 1 / (1 + Defects per unit area × Die area)^N

    What each symbol means, in plain words:

    • Wafer yield: the fraction of wafers that aren't completely bad. Usually taken as 1 (100%).
    • Defects per unit area (D): how many "dust specks" land per cm², about 0.016–0.057 per cm² (2010).
    • N, process-complexity factor: how hard the manufacturing process is; 11.5–15.5 for 40 nm. The professor uses N = 13.5.

    § 1.8Dependability: How Reliable Is the System?

    The big ideaA system is either working as promised or broken. We measure how long it works before breaking (MTTF), how long repairs take (MTTR), and from those, what fraction of the time it's up (availability).

    What is an SLA, and what are the two states?

    A provider promises a level of service in a Service Level Agreement (SLA) or Service Level Objective (SLO), e.g. "the website will be up 99.9% of the time". Compared with that promise, a system is always in one of two states:

    • Service accomplishment: working as the SLA promises.
    • Service interruption: not working as promised.
    • Going from working to broken is a failure. Going from broken back to working is a restoration.

    Reliability vs availability

    Reliability is about how long it keeps working without a break, measured from some starting moment. Availability is what fraction of the time it's working, counting breaks and repairs; a number between 0 and 1.

    MTTF = Mean Time To Failure (measures reliability) FIT = Failures In Time = 1 / MTTF (a rate, reported as failures per 10⁹ hours) MTTR = Mean Time To Repair (measures how long an interruption lasts) MTBF = MTTF + MTTR (mean time between failures) Availability = MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR) = MTTF / MTBF

    If a system is made of many parts and any part failing breaks it, the failure rates add up: FITsystem = sum of the parts' FITs, so MTTFsystem = 1 ÷ Σ(1/MTTFi).

    Worked example

    SetupA disk system has: 10 disks (MTTF 1,000,000 h each), 1 controller (500,000 h), 1 power supply (200,000 h), 1 fan (200,000 h), 1 cable (1,000,000 h).
    Failure rate = 10/1,000,000 + 1/500,000 + 1/200,000 + 1/200,000 + 1/1,000,000 = (10 + 2 + 5 + 5 + 1) / 1,000,000 = 23 / 1,000,000 per hour FIT = 23,000 (failures per 10⁹ h) MTTF_system = 1,000,000 / 23 ≈ 43,500 hours (≈ 5 years) If MTTR = 24 h: Availability = 43,500 / (43,500 + 24) = 0.99945

    The weakest parts (power supply and fan) dominate. The fix is redundancy: with two power supplies, the system fails only if the second one dies before the first is repaired, which raises that pair's MTTF enormously.

    What does this look like in the real world?

    "Five nines" (99.999%) availability allows only about 5.26 minutes of downtime a year. Cloud providers reach it through redundancy (RAID disks, copies of servers, several data centres), not by having perfect parts. As transistors shrink, cosmic rays and ageing cause more random errors, so memories use ECC (error-correcting codes). Also, real parts don't fail at a constant rate: they follow a bathtub curve (many early failures, a calm middle, then wear-out).

    § 1.9Measuring Performance and the Iron Law

    The big ideaThe only honest measure of speed is how long a real program takes to run. That time is always three numbers multiplied together: how many instructions × cycles per instruction × time per cycle. This is the Iron Law. To go faster, you must shrink at least one of the three without growing the others.

    "Faster" can mean two different things

    PlaneWashington → ParisSpeedPassengersThroughput (passenger-mph)
    Boeing 7476.5 h610 mph470286,700

    For one passenger, Concorde is faster: 6.5 ÷ 3 ≈ 2.2× (this is latency or response time). For moving the most people per hour, the 747 wins (this is throughput). A laptop user cares about response time; a data-centre manager cares about throughput.

    Performance(X) = 1 / Execution_time(X) (bigger is better) "X is n times faster than Y": Performance(X) Execution_time(Y) n = ──────────────── = ───────────────── Performance(Y) Execution_time(X)

    The Iron Law

    Time Instructions Cycles Time ───────── = ──────────── × ─────────── × ─────── Program Program Instruction Cycle Texec = IC × CPI × Tclock = IC × CPI / f = IC × (1/IPC) × Tclock
    • IC (instruction count): how many instructions the program runs. Set by the algorithm, the compiler and the ISA.
    • CPI (cycles per instruction): average clock beats per instruction. Set by the ISA and the microarchitecture. (IPC = 1/CPI, instructions per cycle.)
    • Tclock (clock cycle time): length of one beat = 1/f. Set by the microarchitecture and the technology.

    Ways to go faster (the professor's list): cut Tclock (faster technology such as BiCMOS or ECL, smaller transistors, pipelining); cut the instruction count; cut CPI, i.e. raise IPC. CPI below 1 means doing things in parallel (superscalar, or several processors).

    RISC vs CISC through the Iron Law: CISC uses powerful instructions to lower IC, but may raise CPI and Tclock. RISC uses simple instructions for a faster clock and low CPI, but needs more instructions. Only the product of all three matters.

    Worked examples

    Example 1 (the professor's)900 million instructions, CPI = 1.8, cycle time = 10 ns.
    Texec = 900 × 10⁶ × 1.8 × 10 × 10⁻⁹ s = 16.2 s.
    Example 2: average CPI from an instruction mixDifferent instructions take different numbers of cycles. Take a weighted average: multiply each type's share by its cycles, then add.
    Type Freq Cycles Load 25% 2 Store 15% 2 Branch 20% 2 ALU 40% 1 Average CPI = Σ(Freqᵢ × CPIᵢ) / Σ Freqᵢ = 0.25×2 + 0.15×2 + 0.20×2 + 0.40×1 = 1.6
    Example 3: is a "better" instruction really better? (the professor's)Add a new compare-and-branch instruction. Now 25% of branches no longer need a separate ALU instruction before them, so there are fewer instructions. But the clock gets 10% slower.
    New IC = (1 − 0.25×0.2) N = 0.95 N ← 25% of the 20% branches lose an ALU instr New CPI = [0.25×2 + 0.15×2 + 0.2×2 + (0.4 − 0.25×0.2)×1] / 0.95 = 1.55/0.95 = 1.63 Old time = N × 1.6 × T New time = 0.95 N × 1.63 × 1.1 T Speedup = 1.6 / (0.95 × 1.63 × 1.1) = 0.94 → the new CPU is SLOWER

    Lesson: fewer instructions don't guarantee a faster machine. Always check all three terms.

    § 1.10Benchmarks: Fair Tests of Speed

    The big ideaA benchmark is a standard test program used to compare computers fairly. The closer the benchmark is to real work, the more its score means. The best-known standard is SPEC.

    What kinds of benchmark are there?

    The best test is the real application you'll use (Word, Excel, Photoshop). But when buying or designing a machine you often don't know the exact workload, so we use programs that resemble real ones. From most to least trustworthy:

    1. Real applications: the most trustworthy.
    2. Kernels: small, important pieces taken from real programs. Examples: Livermore Loops, LINPACK (LINPACK still ranks the TOP500 supercomputers).
    3. Toy programs: about 100-line beginner exercises. Examples: Sieve of Eratosthenes, Puzzle, Quicksort.
    4. Synthetic benchmarks: made-up programs designed to behave like real ones. Examples: Whetstone (floating point), Dhrystone (integer).

    Who makes the standard benchmark suites?

    SPEC (Standard Performance Evaluation Corporation, spec.org) was set up to make CPU measurement fair and realistic.

    • CPU: SPEC89 → SPEC92 → SPEC95 → SPEC CPU2000 (11 integer + 13 floating-point programs) → CPU2006 → SPEC CPU2017.
    • Graphics: SPECviewperf, SPECapc. Servers: SPECSFS (file server), SPECWeb.
    • PCs: Winbench, Business Winstone, High-end Winstone, CC Winstone.
    • Transaction processing: TPC (tpc.org), e.g. TPC-C, TPC-H. Embedded: EEMBC (eembc.org).
    How SPEC reports a scoreFor each program: SPECratio = (time on a fixed reference machine) ÷ (time on your machine). A SPECratio of 20 means 20× faster than the reference. The ratios of all programs are combined with the geometric mean, so the ranking doesn't depend on which reference machine was chosen.

    What can go wrong with benchmarks?

    People cheat. Compiler writers have added special tricks that only recognise a particular benchmark. Clever compilers could optimise Dhrystone almost to nothing, so synthetic benchmarks lost credibility and SPEC switched to real programs (gcc, perl, video compression). Today MLPerf plays the same role for AI hardware.

    § 1.11Amdahl's Law and Lhadma's Law

    The big ideaIf you speed up only part of a job, the part you didn't touch sets a hard limit. Make 60% of the work infinitely fast and the other 40% is still there, so you can never be more than 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5× faster.

    What does Amdahl's law say exactly?

    It tells you the overall speed-up you get from improving some part of a computer. The overall gain is limited by the fraction of time the improvement can actually be used.

    ExTime_without_E Performance_with_E Speedup(E) = ────────────────── = ────────────────── ExTime_with_E Performance_without_E ExTime_new = ExTime_old × [ (1 − Fraction_enh) + Fraction_enh / Speedup_enh ] ExTime_old 1 Speedup_overall = ───────────── = ─────────────────────────────────────── ExTime_new (1 − Fraction_enh) + Fraction_enh / Speedup_enh

    The two numbers you need:

    • Fractionenhanced = what share of the original time can use the improvement. If 20 s of a 60 s program can use it, F = 20/60.
    • Speedupenhanced = how much faster that part becomes. If that part drops from 5 s to 2 s, S = 5/2.

    Proving it for parallel processors (asked in 2022 and 2023)

    Think of the kitchen from Unit 0: some steps only one chef can do (serial), the rest can be shared among n chefs (parallel).

    1. A program takes time T on one processor. Split it into a serial part S and a parallel part P:  T = S + P … (1)
    2. On n processors, only P is shared out:  Tₙ = S + P/n … (2)
    3. Measure time so that the total is 1:  S + P = 1 ⇒ S = 1 − P … (3)
    4. Put (3) into (2):  Tₙ = (1 − P) + P/n … (4). Here (1 − P) is the serial part and P/n is the shared part.
    5. Speedup = old time ÷ new time = T ÷ Tₙ, so  Speedup = 1 / [ (1 − P) + P/n ] … (5)
    6. The limit: with endless processors, P/n → 0, so Speedup_max = 1 / (1 − P). If 95% is parallel, the best possible is 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20×, even with a million processors.

    Worked examples

    Example 1 (the professor's)A new processor is 5× faster but can be used only 60% of the time.
    Speedup = 1 / (0.4 + 0.6/5) = 1 / (0.4 + 0.12) = 1 / 0.52 ≈ 1.92.
    Example 2: web server (the professor's)New CPU is 10× faster at computing. The old CPU was busy computing 40% of the time and waiting for I/O 60% of the time.
    Speedup = 1 / (0.6 + 0.4/10) = 1 / 0.64 ≈ 1.56. A 10× faster CPU gives barely 1.5×.
    PYQ 2022 Q2(a): "Bob's speedup"

    Bob must get a speedup of 3.8 on 4 processors. He makes the program 95% parallel. Assuming the same problem size and ignoring communication costs, what speedup does he actually get?

    P = 0.95, n = 4 Speedup = 1 / [(1 − 0.95) + 0.95/4] = 1 / (0.05 + 0.2375) = 1 / 0.2875 = 3.48 Bob gets 3.48 < 3.8, so no pay raise. Parallel fraction actually needed for 3.8: 3.8 = 1/[(1−P) + P/4] ⇒ P = (1 − 1/3.8)/(1 − 1/4) = 0.9825 → 98.25% parallel

    What does it teach designers? And what is Lhadma's law?

    • Use Amdahl's law to decide which improvement is worth making.
    • Make the common case fast: a small improvement to something that happens a lot beats a big improvement to something rare.
    • Lhadma's law ("Amdahl" spelled backwards): while making the common case fast, don't make the rare case slower. Example 3 in § 1.9 is exactly this: a "better" branch instruction slowed the clock for every instruction.
    • Diminishing returns: once the easy gains are taken, each extra effort gives less and less, eventually close to nothing.

    Where does Amdahl's law fall short?

    It assumes the problem size stays fixed. Gustafson's law (1988) points out that with more processors we usually solve a bigger problem in the same time: a weather model uses a finer grid rather than finishing the same grid sooner. Then the scaled speedup = n − (1 − P)(n − 1), which grows almost in line with n. Amdahl also ignores the time processors spend communicating and waiting for each other, so real speedups are even lower. Its lasting lesson: the serial part, not the number of cores, sets the limit.

    § 2.1The Memory Hierarchy: Why Memory Comes in Levels

    The big ideaFast memory is expensive, and cheap memory is slow. So we stack them: a tiny fast memory near the CPU, bigger slower ones further away. Because programs keep reusing the same data (locality), the CPU almost always finds what it needs in the fast level. The whole thing then feels as fast as the top and as big as the bottom.

    What is it, exactly?

    A memory hierarchy arranges storage into several levels of different speed, size and cost per bit, so the processor sees memory that is almost as fast as the fastest level and almost as big and cheap as the biggest. (Remember the kitchen in Unit 0: hands → counter → fridge → supermarket.)

    It has two main parts:

    • Internal / primary memory: registers, cache and main memory. The CPU can reach these directly.
    • External / secondary memory: magnetic disk, SSD, optical disk, tape. The CPU reaches these through an I/O module.

    How do the levels differ? (the professor's four characteristics)

    1. Capacity: how much it holds. Grows as you go down the pyramid.
    2. Access time: the wait between asking for data and getting it. Also grows going down.
    3. Performance: without a hierarchy, the CPU would wait on slow memory all the time. The less often you have to go far down the pyramid, the faster the computer.
    4. Cost per bit: grows as you go up. Internal memory costs more than external.

    Why we need it (the professor's list): it spreads data across levels simply and cheaply; removes external fragmentation; allows demand paging and pre-paging; makes swapping efficient.

    Why does it work? Locality

    Example: studying for an examYou keep today's chapter open on your desk (you'll look at it again in a minute: temporal locality). When you open page 40, you'll probably read page 41 next (spatial locality). So keeping the current book on your desk, not in the library, saves you almost every trip.

    Programs behave the same way. A common rule: a program spends 90% of its time in 10% of its code (the 90/10 rule).

    • Temporal locality (time): something used recently will probably be used again soon: a loop counter, a function called again and again. Caches use this by keeping recently used data.
    • Spatial locality (space): things stored next to each other are usually used close together in time: walking through an array, instructions one after another. Caches use this by fetching a whole block of neighbours at once.

    The professor's recipe: keep everything on disk; copy recently used (and nearby) items into the smaller DRAM main memory; copy the most recently used (and nearby) items into the even smaller SRAM cache next to the CPU.

    The three properties: inclusion, coherence, locality

    Kai Hwang's textbook (a prescribed book) says a hierarchy M1 (closest to the CPU) … Mn must have:

    • Inclusion: M1 ⊂ M2 ⊂ … ⊂ Mn. Whatever is in an upper level is also in every level below it. If you miss at level i, you look in level i+1.
    • Coherence: copies of the same item on different levels must agree. Kept by write-through (update the lower level straight away) or write-back (update it when the item is thrown out).
    • Locality: temporal, spatial and sequential (instructions run in order unless there's a jump). This is what makes the hierarchy work.

    How did memory systems evolve to close the processor–memory gap? (asked in 2023)

    Since 1980, processors got 25–52% faster each year but DRAM latency only about 7% faster. The gap (the "memory wall") grew past 1000×. How designers responded:

    1. 1960s–70s: core memory, then semiconductor DRAM; the first cache (IBM System/360 Model 85, 1968).
    2. 1980s: L1 cache on the CPU chip (Intel 486, 8 KB); separate instruction and data caches.
    3. 1990s: L2 caches (first off-chip, then on-chip); non-blocking caches; hardware prefetching; SDRAM.
    4. 2000s: shared L3 for multicore chips; DDR memory generations; memory controller moved onto the CPU (AMD K8, Intel Nehalem).
    5. 2010s–now: huge last-level caches (AMD 3D V-Cache stacks 64 MB on the chip); HBM stacked memory for GPUs; NUMA; SSD/NVMe instead of disks; persistent memory.

    The trade-offs they faced:

    • Size vs speed: a bigger cache misses less but is slower and uses more power.
    • Associativity vs hit time (§ 2.2).
    • Block size: bigger blocks use spatial locality but make each miss cost more.
    • More levels: lower average access time, but more complexity, area and coherence traffic.
    • Write-through vs write-back: simplicity vs saving bandwidth.
    • Chip area: caches take over half of a modern die, space not spent on cores.
    • Cost and power: SRAM needs 6 transistors per bit and leaks.

    Critical point: it all depends on locality. Programs with poor locality (graph analytics, pointer chasing, big scans) break the illusion, and then memory speed dominates.

    § 2.2How a Cache Works: Hits, Misses and Mapping

    The big ideaThe CPU always checks the cache first. Found it: a hit (fast). Not there: a miss, so the block is fetched from main memory and a copy is kept in the cache. Mapping is the rule for which cache slot a block is allowed to go into: exactly one slot (direct), any slot (fully associative), or any slot within one small group (set associative).

    Hits, misses and average access time

    Hit ratio (h) = hits / (hits + misses) = hits / total accesses Miss rate = 1 − h AMAT (average memory access time) = Hit time + Miss rate × Miss penalty
    ExampleHit time 1 ns, miss rate 5%, miss penalty 100 ns.
    AMAT = 1 + 0.05 × 100 = 6 ns. Even a 5% miss rate makes memory 6× slower than a pure cache hit.

    Main memory is divided into equal-size blocks. The cache is divided into lines of the same size (each line holds one block). Blocks are copied into the cache; the original stays in main memory.

    The three mapping methods

    Example: parking a car Direct mapped: your car must go in bay number (your plate number MOD number of bays). If someone's there, they get kicked out.
    Fully associative: park in any free bay. Finding your car later means checking every bay.
    Set associative: the car park has rows (sets). Your plate decides the row; you can park in any bay in that row.
    1. Direct mapping

    Each block can go into only one particular line:

    Cache line number = (Main-memory block address) MOD (Number of lines in cache) Physical address = | TAG | LINE NUMBER (index) | BLOCK OFFSET | rest log2(number of lines) log2(block size)
    • Offset says which byte inside the block.
    • Index / line says which cache line to look in.
    • Tag is the rest of the address, stored with the line so we can check it's really our block and not another one that maps to the same line.

    No choice to make, so no replacement algorithm is needed. Only one tag comparison (one comparator).

    2. Fully associative mapping

    A block can go into any line. The address is just | TAG | OFFSET | (no index). Every lookup compares the tag with all stored tags at once (one comparator per line, "content-addressable memory"). Needs a replacement rule (LRU, FIFO, random).

    3. k-way set-associative mapping

    Lines are grouped into sets of k lines. A block maps to exactly one set, and can use any of the k lines in it:

    Number of sets = Number of lines / k Set number = (Block address) MOD (Number of sets) Address = | TAG | SET INDEX (log2 sets) | OFFSET (log2 block size) |

    k comparators check the chosen set in parallel. Direct mapped is just 1-way; fully associative is one set containing every line.

    What happens on a write? And who gets thrown out?

    • Write-through: every write updates the cache and memory. Simple, memory is always correct, but lots of memory traffic, so a write buffer is added.
    • Write-back: writes only update the cache; the line is marked dirty and written to memory only when it is thrown out. Less traffic, but memory can be out of date (a coherence issue, § 2.7).
    • On a write miss: write-allocate (bring the block into the cache first; usually with write-back) or no-write-allocate (write straight to memory; usually with write-through).
    • Replacement: LRU (throw out the least recently used; best but costly), FIFO (oldest first), random (cheap, nearly as good when k is large).

    Critical point: more associativity lowers the miss rate but slows every hit, and "execution time is the only final measure". So real chips use a small, fast, lower-associativity L1 and bigger, more associative L2/L3.

    § 2.4Making Caches Faster: the Three C's and Six Basic Optimisations

    The big ideaAMAT = Hit time + Miss rate × Miss penalty. So there are only three levers: miss less often, make each miss cheaper, or make each hit quicker. Every cache trick pulls one of these, and usually pushes another the wrong way.
    AMAT = Hit time + Miss rate × Miss penalty CPUtime = IC × (CPI_execution + Mem accesses per instruction × Miss rate × Miss penalty) × Clock cycle time Misses per instruction = Memory accesses per instruction × Miss rate

    Why do misses happen? The three C's

    Example: your study deskCompulsory: the first time you need a book, it can't already be on your desk.
    Capacity: your desk is too small for all the books you need this week, so some go back and get fetched again.
    Conflict: you have a rule that "maths books go on the left corner only", and two maths books keep pushing each other off, even though the right corner is empty.
    Miss typeCauseDefined as the misses in…Fix
    Compulsory (cold-start)First ever use of a block…an infinite cacheBigger blocks, prefetching
    CapacityThe cache can't hold everything the program needs…a fully associative cache of the same sizeBigger cache

    A fourth C, coherence misses, happens in multicore chips when another core's write wipes out your copy (§ 2.7). In the professor's charts, compulsory misses are tiny, capacity misses dominate and fall as the cache grows, and conflict misses shrink as associativity goes from 1-way to 8-way.

    1 · Larger block size

    One miss brings in the neighbours too, which will probably be used soon (spatial locality). But for the same cache size, bigger blocks mean fewer blocks, so more capacity and conflict misses, and each miss moves more bytes. So it helps up to a point, then hurts. In the professor's curve, a 1 KB cache is best at about 32 B blocks and gets worse by 256 B; a 256 KB cache keeps improving up to about 128 B.

    2 · Larger cache

    Cuts capacity and conflict misses (the chart falls steeply from 1 KB to 128 KB), but hits get slower and it costs more power and money. That's why L1 stays small (32–64 KB) and the big capacity goes into L2/L3.

    3 · Higher associativity

    The 2:1 cache rule: a direct-mapped cache of size N misses about as often as a 2-way set-associative cache of size N/2. There is also theory behind it (Sleator & Tarjan, CACM 1985). But execution time is the only final measure. Hill (1988) found the hit time about 10% higher for 2-way than for 1-way.     5 · Giving read misses priority over writes

    The CPU is usually waiting for a read, but not for a write. So let reads jump the queue.

    • Write-through caches park writes in a write buffer. A read miss might need a value that's still sitting in that buffer (a read-after-write clash). Simply waiting for the buffer to empty can add about 50% to the read-miss penalty (old MIPS 1000). Better: check the write buffer on a read, and if there's no clash, let the read go first.
    • Write-back caches: a read miss that throws out a dirty block would normally write the dirty block first, then read. Better: copy the dirty block into a buffer, do the read, then do the write. The CPU restarts as soon as the read finishes.
    6 · Avoiding address translation while indexing the cache

    Programs use virtual addresses that must be translated to physical ones (§ 2.8), which takes time. Idea: use the virtual address directly to look up the cache (a virtually addressed cache). Problems:

    • Process switch: another program uses the same virtual addresses for different data, so the cache must be flushed or it gives false hits (cost: flush time + a cold, empty cache). Fix: add a process-identifier tag (PID) so a hit also needs the right process.
    • Aliases (synonyms): two virtual addresses for the same physical data could sit in two lines. Fix: hardware forces the index bits to match, called page colouring.
    • I/O devices use physical addresses.

    The usual modern compromise is VIPT (virtually indexed, physically tagged): pick the set using the page-offset bits (which are the same in virtual and physical addresses), while the TLB translates in parallel. Example: Intel Core L1 data cache, 48 KB, 12-way: 48 KB ÷ 12 = 4 KB per way, exactly one page, so the index comes only from page-offset bits and aliases are impossible.

    § 2.5Advanced Cache Optimisations

    The big ideaBeyond simply making caches bigger, there are cleverer tricks: keep a small bin of recently thrown-out blocks (victim cache), check a second spot before giving up (pseudo-associative), fetch data before it's asked for (prefetching), and rearrange the program itself so it uses the cache better (compiler optimisations).
    1 · Victim cache (fewer conflict misses, cheaper misses)

    A tiny, fully associative buffer that holds blocks recently thrown out of the cache. On a miss, check the victim cache before going to memory; if it's there, swap it back. Like a recycling bin you can still pull things out of.

    Key fact: Jouppi (1990) found that a 4-entry victim cache removed 20%–95% of conflict misses for a 4 KB direct-mapped data cache. Used in Alpha and HP PA-RISC machines. You get direct-mapped speed with some of the conflict protection of associativity.

    2 · Pseudo-associative (column-associative) cache

    Aims for the fast hit of direct mapping plus the fewer conflicts of 2-way. Split the cache in two halves. On a miss, check the matching line in the other half; if the block is there, that's a pseudo-hit (a slower hit). The simple way to find the other line: flip the most significant bit of the index.

    Timing: hit time < pseudo-hit time < miss penalty. Drawback: a hit that sometimes takes 1 cycle and sometimes 2 upsets the CPU pipeline, so it's better for caches not directly feeding the CPU (L2). Used in the MIPS R10000 L2 and similarly in UltraSPARC.

    3 · Hardware prefetching

    Fetch instructions or data before the CPU asks, into the cache or a separate buffer. Example: on a miss, the Alpha AXP 21064 fetches two blocks: the requested one into the cache and the next one into an instruction stream buffer. The same works for data. With four data stream buffers, the data hit rate improved by 43%; in some cases eight stream buffers caught 50–70% of all misses. It needs spare memory bandwidth, and wrong guesses waste bandwidth and can push useful data out.

    4 · Compiler optimisations (no new hardware at all)

    The compiler rearranges code and data so they have better locality:

    • Reorder procedures in memory so they don't fight over the same cache lines.
    • Merging arrays: store related values side by side (one array of pairs instead of 2 arrays), for better spatial locality.
    • Loop interchange: swap the order of nested loops so memory is walked in the order it's stored.
    • Loop fusion: join two loops that go over the same data into one.
    • Blocking: work on small square tiles of a matrix that fit in the cache, reusing them many times before moving on.
    Loop interchange in one pictureC stores a 2-D array row by row. Reading it column by column jumps 100 elements each step, like reading a book by taking the first word of every page. Swapping the loops reads along the rows, like reading normally, so every block fetched is fully used.

    § 2.7Cache Coherence and Synchronisation

    The big ideaIn a multicore chip, every core has its own private cache. If two cores copy the same variable and one changes it, the other is left holding an out-of-date copy. Cache coherence is the set of rules that makes sure every core always sees the latest value, usually by telling the others to throw away their old copy.

    What is the problem?

    Example: a shared shopping listYou and your flatmate each photocopy the shopping list. You cross out "milk" on your copy because you bought it. Your flatmate still sees "milk" on their copy and buys it again. Two copies, one changed, nobody told the other: that is the coherence problem.

    Definition (the professor's): the challenge of keeping multiple local caches synchronised when one processor updates its local copy of data that is shared among multiple caches.

    Why does it happen?

    The professor's example: in a dual-core processor, both cores load the same memory block into their private caches. One core writes a new value. When the other core reads that location from its own cache, it gets the old value, unless its copy is invalidated so it misses and fetches the new one. Without a coherence rule, programs read wrong data and may crash.

    Three causes: (1) sharing data that gets written, (2) a process moving from one core to another, (3) I/O (DMA) writing memory behind the caches' backs.

    Both write policies have the problem:

    • Write-back only updates the cache, so memory and other caches are out of date. Clearly inconsistent.
    • Write-through updates memory too, so memory is fine, but other caches still hold stale copies unless they watch memory traffic or are told directly.

    How is it solved?

    A · Write-through protocols
    • Write-through with update: write the new value to your cache and to memory, and broadcast the new value to all other processors. Any cache holding that block updates it.
    • Write-through with invalidation: write to memory and broadcast an invalidate message. Every other cache holding the block drops it.
    B · Write-back protocol (ownership)

    Many caches can hold copies as long as they only read. To change the block, a processor must first become its exclusive owner. Ownership is granted by the block's home memory module, and all other copies, including memory's, are invalidated. The owner can then change it freely. When someone else wants to read it, the owner sends the data to them and to home memory, which takes back ownership and stores the latest value. (Like a library book: many people can look, but only the borrower can write in it, and it must be returned before anyone else borrows it.)

    C · Software solutions

    The compiler analyses the code, finds data that could be unsafe to cache, and marks it non-cacheable (or adds flush/invalidate instructions). The cost is paid at compile time, but it's cautious and uses the cache less.

    D · Hardware: directory protocols

    Keep a central record (a directory) of which caches hold a copy of each line. It lives in main memory and is run by a central controller. When a cache wants to change its copy:

    1. The cache asks the central controller for exclusive access to the line.
    2. The controller messages every processor with a copy, telling each to invalidate it.
    3. It waits for an acknowledgement from each one.
    4. It grants exclusive access to the requester.
    5. Later, if another processor tries to read that line, it misses and tells the controller.
    6. The controller tells the owner to write back to memory, and the line becomes shared again.

    Downsides: the central controller is a bottleneck, and there's communication overhead. Upside: no broadcasting, so it scales to many processors (large NUMA systems use distributed directories).

    E · Hardware: snoopy protocols

    No central record. Every cache controller listens ("snoops") to a shared bus. When a shared line is updated, the update is broadcast, and every other cache reacts. Ideal for bus-based multiprocessors, because a bus is naturally a broadcast channel.

    What happens in a write-invalidate snoopy protocol (states: Valid, Reserved, Dirty, Invalid)
    • Read-miss: start a bus read. If no cache has a dirty copy, memory supplies the block; if some cache has a dirty copy, that cache blocks memory and supplies it. The new copy is valid.
    • Write-hit: if the copy is dirty or reserved, just write locally; it becomes dirty. If it's valid, broadcast an invalidate to all caches; after this first write-through to memory, the state is reserved.
    • Write-miss: get the block from memory or from the cache holding it dirty, broadcast a read-invalidate to kill all other copies, then update the local copy as dirty.
    • Read-hit: always served locally, no bus use, no state change.
    • Block replacement: a dirty copy must be written back to memory; valid, reserved or invalid copies don't need to be.

    Synchronisation: taking turns safely

    Coherence keeps copies consistent. Synchronisation decides who goes when, e.g. only one core updates a bank balance at a time. Hardware provides atomic read-modify-write instructions that can't be interrupted halfway: test-and-set, atomic exchange, fetch-and-increment, compare-and-swap, and the pair load-linked / store-conditional (LL/SC, in MIPS, ARM and RISC-V). Built on them:

    • Spin lock: keep checking a lock variable until it's free. The smart version ("test-and-test-and-set") spins on its own cached copy and only tries the atomic swap when coherence tells it the lock changed, which cuts bus traffic.
    • Barrier: every thread waits until all threads reach the same point.
    • Semaphore: a counter controlling how many threads may enter.

    Memory-consistency models (sequential consistency vs relaxed models with fences) define what ordering of reads and writes other cores are guaranteed to see.

    Real world, and a trap: false sharing

    Modern chips mix both approaches. Intel's L3 keeps "core valid" bits recording which cores might have a line, so invalidations go only to those cores (a directory inside a snoopy design). False sharing: two threads write to different variables that happen to sit in the same 64-byte cache line. The line keeps bouncing between their caches, which can make a program 10× slower. Fix: pad the variables onto separate lines.

    Critical point: snooping is simple and fast but doesn't scale (broadcasting to every core gets expensive; bus systems top out around 8–32 cores). Directories scale but need extra storage (a bit per processor per block) and add delay. Software schemes avoid hardware cost but are cautious. Real designs snoop inside a chip and use directories between chips.

    § 2.8Virtual Memory

    The big ideaEvery program is given the illusion of its own huge, private memory. Behind the scenes, memory is cut into fixed-size pages. The pages in use sit in RAM; the rest wait on disk. A page table translates each program address to where the data really is, and a small fast cache of translations, the TLB, makes that translation quick.

    What is virtual memory?

    The professor's definition: a technique that lets users run programs that need more memory than the computer physically has. "Virtual" means something that appears to be there but actually isn't. When physical memory runs out, the computer writes what it needs to keep onto the disk (a swap file) and uses that as extra memory.

    Example: a hotel with room numbersEvery guest is told "your room is number 5". The front desk has a book (the page table) that says guest A's "room 5" is really room 312, and guest B's "room 5" is really room 118. Some guests' luggage is kept in storage in the basement (the disk) until they need it.
    • Virtual (logical) address: the address a program uses. All of them together are the address space.
    • Physical address: an actual location in RAM. All of them together are the memory space.

    Why is address translation necessary? (asked in 2022)

    1. Size: the address space can be far bigger than RAM (64-bit addresses vs 16 GB RAM), so something must track which pages are currently in which RAM frames.
    2. Relocation: every program is compiled as if it starts at address 0. Translation lets it be placed anywhere in RAM, even in scattered pieces.
    3. Protection: each process has its own page table, so it can't reach another's memory. Permission bits enforce read/write/execute rights.
    4. Sharing: two processes' pages can point to the same frame (shared libraries).
    5. Demand paging: only pages actually used are loaded, so programs start fast and more of them fit at once.

    How does it work? (the professor's five-step diagram)

    1. The CPU produces a logical address: a logical page number + a position (x) inside the page.
    2. The operating system's mapper must turn it into a real (physical) main-memory address.
    3. If the page is in main memory, the CPU gets the data from there.
    4. If the mapper finds the page is not in main memory, a page fault happens,
    5. and the page is read from disk into a free frame in main memory (steps 4 and 5).

    Only the page number is translated. The offset (the position inside the page) is copied across unchanged, just as "seat 14" stays "seat 14" whichever carriage you're moved to.

    • Page table: maps each VPN to its frame number (PFN). Valid = 1: the page is in RAM. Valid = 0: page fault, and the OS brings it from disk.
    • PTE bits: dirty (page was changed, so write it back when evicted), referenced (used recently; helps LRU), protection bits.
    • TLB (Translation Lookaside Buffer): the page table lives in memory, so every access would need two memory trips (first the PTE, then the data). The TLB is a small, fast, usually fully associative cache of recent translations (VPN → PFN + bits), typically 64–1536 entries.

    How much does the TLB help? With TLB hit rate α, TLB time t and memory time m:

    Effective Access Time (EAT) = α (t + m) + (1 − α)(t + 2m) Example: α = 0.98, t = 1 ns, m = 100 ns EAT = 0.98 × 101 + 0.02 × 201 = 98.98 + 4.02 = 103 ns (vs 200 ns without a TLB, a 1.94× speedup)

    TLB hits are common because of locality: one entry covers a whole 4 KB page (1024 words). Bigger pages (2 MB or 1 GB "huge pages") increase TLB reach. On a process switch, the TLB is flushed or its entries are tagged with an ASID (address-space ID).

    Page replacement: which page gets kicked out?

    When a page fault happens and there's no free frame, a page replacement algorithm chooses a victim:

    • FIFO: evict the page that's been in memory longest. Easy (take the front of the queue), but may evict a busy page, so pages get reloaded often. Suffers Belady's anomaly: more frames can sometimes cause more faults.
    • LRU (Least Recently Used): evict the page not used for the longest time. Implemented with a counter per page: reset to 0 when used, and all counters go up at fixed intervals; the highest count is the LRU page. These counters are called aging registers.
    • OPT (Optimal): evict the page that won't be needed for the longest time in the future. Fewest faults possible, but impossible in practice because the OS can't see the future. Used as a yardstick for the others.

    § 2.9Virtual Machines

    The big ideaA virtual machine is a pretend computer made by software. A hypervisor (Virtual Machine Monitor) lets several operating systems share one real computer, each believing it has the hardware to itself. It is virtual memory's idea taken all the way: pretend everything, not just memory.

    How does it work?

    The hypervisor runs in the most privileged mode of the CPU; the guest operating systems run with fewer privileges. When a guest tries a privileged instruction (like changing the page table), it traps to the hypervisor, which does the job safely on its behalf: "trap-and-emulate".

    ExampleA landlord (hypervisor) splits one house (the real computer) into flats. Each tenant (guest OS) thinks they have a whole home. When a tenant wants to rewire the electrics (a privileged action), they must ask the landlord, who does it without affecting the other flats.

    Types and hardware support

    • Type 1 (bare-metal): the hypervisor runs directly on the hardware: VMware ESXi, Xen, Microsoft Hyper-V, KVM. Used in cloud data centres.
    • Type 2 (hosted): runs as an app on a normal OS: VirtualBox, VMware Workstation.
    • Paravirtualisation: the guest OS is modified to call the hypervisor directly (Xen), avoiding instructions that are hard to virtualise.
    • What the ISA must provide (Popek & Goldberg): every sensitive instruction must be privileged, so it traps. Old x86 broke this rule (17 sensitive but unprivileged instructions), so Intel added VT-x and AMD added AMD-V.
    • Memory: two translations now: guest-virtual → guest-physical → host-physical. First done in software with shadow page tables, now in hardware with nested / extended page tables (Intel EPT, AMD NPT), which puts extra pressure on the TLB.

    Real world: AWS EC2 runs customer machines on its Nitro hypervisor. Benefits: isolation (security), consolidation (many small workloads on one server), live migration and quick setup. Costs: a few percent CPU overhead (more for I/O) and costlier TLB misses. Containers (Docker) are a lighter alternative that share the host's kernel: less overhead, less isolation.

    Includes cumulative cards from Unit One (↺).

    Four characteristics of the memory hierarchy?
    Capacity (increases downward), access time (increases downward), performance (minimise how far down you go), cost per bit (increases upward).
    Temporal vs spatial locality?
    Temporal: recently accessed items are likely to be accessed again soon (loops) → keep them in cache. Spatial: nearby addresses are referenced close together in time (arrays) → fetch whole blocks. 90/10 rule.
    The three hierarchy properties (Hwang)?
    Inclusion (M1 ⊂ M2 ⊂ … Mn), Coherence (copies consistent across levels, via write-through or write-back), Locality (temporal, spatial, sequential).
    Direct-mapping line formula and address split?
    Line = block address MOD number of lines. Address = Tag | Line | Offset. One comparator; no replacement algorithm needed.
    PYQ 16-way cache: tag, index, offset?
    Half-word = 4 B is the addressable unit. Block = 128 B = 32 units → offset 5. Lines = 2 MB/128 B = 2¹⁴; sets = 2¹⁰ → index 10. Tag = 64 − 15 = 49.
    2023 Q5b: address 245,678 in a 64 KB, 32 B, 4-way cache?
    512 sets, 4 lines/set; offset 5, index 9, tag 18 bits. Offset = 14, index = 509, tag = 14 (check: 14×16384 + 509×32 + 14 = 245,678).
    PYQ AMAT: hit 5 ns, miss 20 ns, h = 0.75?
    Simultaneous: 0.75×5 + 0.25×20 = 8.75 ns. Hierarchical: 5 + 0.25×20 = 10 ns. State the model used.
    The three C's of misses, defined precisely?
    Compulsory: misses in an infinite cache. Capacity: misses in a fully associative cache of size X. Conflict: extra misses in an N-way cache of size X.
    Six basic cache optimisations?
    Larger block size · larger cache · higher associativity · more cache levels · read priority over writes · avoid address translation during indexing.
    2:1 cache rule?
    Miss rate of a direct-mapped cache of size N ≈ miss rate of a 2-way cache of size N/2 (Sleator & Tarjan). But execution time is the final measure: 2-way hit time is about 10% higher (Hill).
    Local vs global miss rate?
    Local = misses in this cache / accesses to this cache. Global = misses in this cache / CPU accesses = MR_L1 × MR_L2.
    Victim cache: what and how effective?
    A small fully associative buffer of blocks recently evicted, checked on a miss before memory. Jouppi 1990: 4 entries removed 20–95% of conflicts for a 4 KB direct-mapped cache.
    Five compiler cache optimisations?
    Reorder procedures, merging arrays, loop interchange, loop fusion, blocking (capacity misses 2N³+N² → 2N³/B+N²).
    Why is write-through not enough for coherence?
    Memory is updated, but other caches still hold stale copies unless they snoop the traffic or receive invalidate or update messages.
    Directory protocol steps and drawbacks?
    Request exclusive → controller invalidates all copies → acks → grant → another reader misses → owner writes back. Drawbacks: central controller bottleneck, communication overhead. Scales without broadcast.
    Write-invalidate vs write-update snoopy?
    Invalidate: many readers, one writer; invalidate others on write (MESI). Update: many readers and writers; broadcast the new word to all copies (more traffic).
    What does a TLB do, and the EAT formula?
    Caches recent VPN→PFN translations (small, fully associative). EAT = α(t+m) + (1−α)(t+2m). With α = 0.98, t = 1, m = 100: 103 ns vs 200 ns.
    FIFO / LRU / OPT faults for 7,0,1,2,0,3,0,4,2,3,0,3,2 (3 frames)?
    FIFO 10, LRU 9, OPT 7. OPT is optimal but impossible to implement; it is the benchmark.
    How does memory latency enter the Iron Law?
    Through CPI: CPI = CPI_execution + memory accesses/instr × miss rate × miss penalty. Caches reduce the stall term.
    Which Unit 1 trend makes caches necessary?
    Bandwidth vs latency: memory latency improved only 6–8× while processor bandwidth improved 10,000–25,000×. The memory wall.
    Apply Amdahl: a cache speeds up memory access 10×, and memory is 40% of time. Overall speedup?
    1 / (0.6 + 0.4/10) = 1/0.64 = 1.5625×.
    Why are larger dies costly (cost of an IC)?
    Fewer dies per wafer and lower Bose-Einstein yield 1/(1+D·A)^N, so die cost ∝ area⁴. This is one reason cache area is precious.
