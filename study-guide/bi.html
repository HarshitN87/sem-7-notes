    Unit Zero · Before We Begin

    Business and Data from Zero

    How a business works, what data is, and why businesses keep two very different kinds of database, explained with a tea shop.

    Not a syllabus unit by itself, but every idea here (departments, business process, data vs information, table, row, column, transaction, report, structured and unstructured data) appears in Units 1–3 and in exam answers. Read it once slowly. It takes about 20 minutes.

    Step 0.1How a business is organised: from tea stall to company

    The big ideaA business is a group of people doing different jobs (functions) that link together into chains of steps (processes) to deliver something a customer pays for. IT exists to make those jobs and chains faster, cheaper and better informed.

    Imagine Ravi's tea stall. On day one, Ravi does everything himself: buys milk, makes tea, takes money, counts the cash. Ten years later "Ravi's Chai" has 50 shops, and the jobs have split into departments:

    Function (department)What it does at Ravi's Chai
    Purchasing / supply chainBuys milk, tea leaves and cups from suppliers
    Operations / productionMakes the tea in every shop
    Sales & marketingSells tea, runs offers, handles customers
    Finance & accountsTracks money in and out, pays bills, files taxes
    Human resources (HR)Hires, trains and pays staff

    A business process is a sequence of steps that crosses these departments to get something done.

    Example: "order to cash"Customer orders a tea → shop makes it → customer pays → cash is recorded → accounts adds it to the day's sales. Five steps, three departments, one process.
    New words
    Enterprise
    A business organisation, usually a large one
    Function
    One kind of job done in the business (sales, finance, HR…)
    Business process
    A chain of steps, often across departments, that produces a result for a customer

    Step 0.2Data, information, knowledge

    The big ideaData is raw facts. Information is data organised so it means something. Knowledge is understanding what to do about it. Business intelligence is the machinery that climbs this ladder.
    ExampleData: "Shop 12, 7:05 am, 1 masala chai, ₹20" (thousands of lines like this).
    Information: "Shop 12 sells 40% of its tea between 7 and 9 am."
    Knowledge / decision: "Put two staff on the morning shift at Shop 12 and one in the afternoon."
    New words
    Data
    Raw, unorganised facts and figures
    Information
    Data processed and organised so it is meaningful
    Knowledge
    Understanding gained from information, used to make decisions

    Step 0.3What a database is: tables, rows and columns

    The big ideaA database stores data in tables, like a set of very disciplined spreadsheets. Each row is one thing (one sale), each column is one fact about it (time, item, price). You ask it questions in a language called SQL.

    • The primary key (sale_id) is a column whose value is different for every row, so each row can be found exactly.
    • A question in SQL looks like: SELECT SUM(price) FROM sales WHERE shop = 12; meaning "total sales at shop 12".

    Table, row, column

    Schema

    Primary key

    SQL

    Step 0.4Two jobs for data: running the business vs understanding it

    The big ideaBusinesses use data for two very different jobs. Running the business means recording each sale the moment it happens: lots of tiny, fast updates. Understanding the business means asking big questions over years of data: few, slow, heavy reads. They need different systems: OLTP for the first, OLAP and the data warehouse for the second (Unit 3).
    Running the business (OLTP)Understanding the business (OLAP)
    At Ravi's ChaiThe billing machine at the counterThe owner's monthly report and dashboard
    Typical question"Record this ₹20 sale." "Is Shop 12 out of milk?""Which shops grew fastest in the last 3 years, by month and by product?"
    WorkThousands of tiny inserts and updates per minuteA few huge read-only queries over millions of rows
    DataCurrent, detailedHistorical, summarised
    UsersCashiers, clerksManagers, analysts

    Why not use one system? Because the owner's giant 3-year report would slow down every cashier's billing. So data is copied regularly from the OLTP systems into a separate data warehouse, where it is cleaned and arranged for analysis. That copying is called ETL (Extract, Transform, Load).

    The data cube in one lineOLAP thinks of sales as a cube with three sides (dimensions): shop × product × month. Each little box holds a number (a measure, e.g. total sales). "Slicing" the cube means fixing one side ("only March"); "drilling down" means going from year to month to day.
    New words
    Transaction
    One small unit of business work recorded in the database (a sale, a payment)
    OLTP
    On-Line Transaction Processing: systems that record day-to-day transactions
    OLAP
    On-Line Analytical Processing: systems for analysing large amounts of historical data
    Data warehouse
    A separate store of cleaned, historical data from across the business, built for analysis
    ETL
    Extract, Transform, Load: copying data from source systems into the warehouse
    Dimension / measure
    A way to slice data (shop, product, time) / the number being analysed (sales)

    Step 0.5Structured, semi-structured and unstructured data

    The big ideaSome data fits neatly into tables (structured). Some has labels but no fixed table shape (semi-structured). Most data in the world has no fixed shape at all (unstructured): chats, photos, videos, emails. Unit 2 is about all three.
    TypeEveryday example at Ravi's ChaiEasy to search?

    Semi-structuredAn online order sent as XML or JSON: <order><item>chai</item><qty>2</qty></order>Partly: labels (tags) say what each value is, but orders can have different fields
    UnstructuredCustomer reviews, WhatsApp complaints, CCTV video, photos of receiptsNo: needs text mining, image analysis and so on

    Structured data
    Data in a fixed format of rows and columns
    Semi-structured data
    Data with tags or labels but no rigid schema (XML, JSON, email headers)
    Unstructured data
    Data with no predefined format (text, images, audio, video)
    XML

    Step 0.6So what is "Business Intelligence"?

    The big ideaBusiness Intelligence (BI) is the set of processes and tools that turns a business's raw data into information and decisions: collect it, store it in a warehouse, analyse it (OLAP, reports, dashboards), and act on it.
    1. Collect: transactions from OLTP systems (billing, ERP, CRM), plus outside data.
    2. Integrate and store: ETL into a data warehouse or data marts.
    3. Analyse: OLAP cubes, reports, dashboards, data mining.
    4. Decide and act: managers use the insights, then measure the results, which feeds back into step 1.

    How to use this in the exam: BI questions are often applied ("in your university…", "what according to you…"). Give the definition, then an example from a place you know (your college, a shop, a bank), exactly as the tea shop was used here.

    § 1.1How a business is organised, and the IT applications a university uses

    The big ideaA business is split into functions (departments with specialist jobs) that work together through business processes (chains of steps that cross departments). IT is the glue: it automates the steps and shares one set of data. A university is a business too.

    Functions and processes

    Business enterpriseAn organisation that brings together people, capital, technology and processes to create and deliver products or services to customers. It is divided into functions that cooperate through business processes.
    Core functions (create value)Support functions (keep it running)
    Sales & marketing · Product/service delivery (operations, supply chain) · Research & development · Quality · Customer serviceFinance & accounts · Human resources · Information technology · Legal & compliance · Administration, procurement
    Business processA set of related, structured activities, usually crossing functional boundaries, that turns inputs into an output of value to a customer.
    ProcessSteps

    § 1.2The Malcolm Baldrige framework (asked twice in 2023)

    The big ideaBaldrige is a health check-up for any organisation. It doesn't tell you what to do. It asks seven groups of questions: how you lead, plan, serve customers, use data, treat people and run processes, and what results you get.

    Origin and purpose

    Baldrige Excellence FrameworkA systems-based framework for organisational performance excellence, created by the US Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Improvement Act of 1987 and run by NIST. It has seven categories, core values and a scoring system, and is used for the Baldrige Award and for self-assessment by any organisation (business, hospital, school, non-profit).

    The seven categories

    Memory hook: L-S-C-M-W-O-R, "Lead, Strategise, serve Customers, Measure, care for Workforce, run Operations, get Results".

    #CategoryWhat it asksUniversity example
    1LeadershipHow senior leaders set vision and values, communicate, and act ethically and responsiblyVice-Chancellor's vision, code of ethics, outreach
    2Strategic PlanningHow objectives and action plans are made, deployed, changed and trackedFive-year plan, NIRF ranking targets
    3Customer FocusHow the voice of the customer is heard and used; relationships and satisfactionStudent feedback, recruiter surveys, grievance cell
    4Measurement, Analysis & Knowledge ManagementHow data, information and knowledge are gathered, analysed, managed and used to improve. BI serves this category.ERP data, dashboards on pass %, attendance, placements
    5Workforce FocusHow capability and capacity are assessed; how an engaged, well-developed workforce is builtFaculty development, appraisal, workload balance
    6Process Management (Operations)How key work processes are designed, run and improvedAdmission, exam and placement processes
    7ResultsOutcomes for product, customers, workforce, leadership and finance, compared with benchmarksPlacement %, citations, accreditation grade

    Scoring: 1,000 points. Categories 1–6 are judged on ADLI (Approach, Deployment, Learning, Integration). Results (7) is judged on LeTCI (Levels, Trends, Comparisons, Integration) and carries the most points (450).

    Core values include systems perspective, visionary leadership, customer-focused excellence, valuing people, organisational learning, management by fact, ethics and transparency, and delivering results.

    Strengths and criticisms: it is holistic, links leadership to results and works for any sector. It is also paperwork-heavy, can become box-ticking, and winning does not guarantee success. Related models: EFQM (Europe), India's Rajiv Gandhi National Quality Award, and Tata's TBEM, adapted from Baldrige.

    § 1.3Business Process Management (2023 Q2a)

    The big ideaBPM is continuously improving how work flows: draw the process, run it, measure it, fix it, repeat. It is a loop, not a one-off project.
    Business Process Management (BPM)A discipline that uses methods, tools and technology to discover, model, analyse, measure, improve, automate and optimise business processes continuously, so they stay aligned with business goals. BPR (re-engineering), by contrast, is a one-time radical redesign.

    The lifecycle: the "processes involved in BPM"

    1. Design: find the current ("as-is") process and design the wanted ("to-be") one, with goals, owners and inputs and outputs.
    2. Model: draw it formally, usually in BPMN (events, activities, gateways, swim-lanes), and simulate what-if cases.
    3. Execute: run it in a BPM or workflow tool linked to ERP and CRM, automating rule-based steps (including RPA bots), and train people.
    4. Monitor: track KPIs (cycle time, error rate, cost per transaction) on dashboards. This is where BI plugs in.
    5. Optimise: remove bottlenecks and waste, redesign, and loop back to step 1.

    Supporting activities: governance (process owners), documentation, change management and compliance.

    Example: bank loan approvalAs-is: 7 days, 5 hand-offs. Model it in BPMN. Execute by automating KYC through Aadhaar e-KYC and credit-bureau checks. Monitor turnaround on a dashboard. Optimise with auto-approval for low-risk applicants. Result: most approvals in minutes.

    Caution: automating a bad process only makes a bad process faster, so redesign comes before automation. Without monitoring data (BI), "optimise" is guesswork.

    10-mark answer, 2(a)

    Define process and BPM, and BPM vs BPR (2) → cycle diagram (2) → five stages with 2 lines each (4) → example and the role of BI in monitoring (2).

    § 1.4Why businesses use IT, and the connected world (2023 Q3a)

    The big ideaBusinesses use IT to do work faster, share one version of data, decide with facts, and reach customers anywhere. Today everything is online and linked (the connected world), so business applications must be Internet-ready.

    Key purposes of IT in business

    1. Automate operations: billing, payroll and similar work becomes faster, more accurate and cheaper.
    2. Integrate functions: one shared data source removes silos (ERP).
    3. Support decisions: reports, dashboards and analytics replace gut feel with facts (BI).
    4. Improve customer experience: portals, apps, CRM, personalisation.
    5. Enable collaboration: email, intranets, video calls.
    6. Gain competitive advantage: new digital products and business models.
    7. Compliance and records: audit trails, tax filing.
    8. Reach and scale: global, 24×7 operation.

    The connected world

    The connected world is the environment in which people, organisations, devices and applications are permanently linked through the Internet, mobile networks, cloud and IoT, so information flows anytime, anywhere, on any device. Customers expect to order, pay and track online, and partners expect systems to talk through APIs.

    The one trick for the case-study questions

    Every case study asks the same three things: (1) sort the data into three types, (2) say why the database data is structured, (3) say why the rest is hard to manage and search. Learn the pattern once (§ 1.4) and you can answer all three cases, and any new one the examiner invents.

    § 2.1What is structured data? Characteristics and examples Assignment 1 · Q1

    The big ideaStructured data is data that fits neatly into a table: fixed columns, one row per record, every value of a known type. Because the shape is decided before the data arrives, a computer can find anything in it instantly.

    The definition to write

    Structured dataData that conforms to a predefined data model (schema) and is organised in rows and columns with defined data types, so it can be stored in a relational database and retrieved with SQL.

    Picture a school attendance register. Every page has the same columns (roll no., name, date, present/absent) and every student gets one row. Nobody has to guess what a value means. That register, made digital, is structured data.

    Six characteristics (write at least five)

    1. Follows a data model. The design (schema) is fixed first, and every record has the same attributes.
    2. Rows and columns. Each column has a data type (INT, VARCHAR, DATE) and a size.
    3. Similar things are grouped. All students in a Student table, all courses in a Course table.
    4. Relationships are explicit. A primary key identifies a row and a foreign key links one table to another.
    5. Constraints protect quality. NOT NULL, UNIQUE, CHECK and referential integrity stop bad data from getting in.
    6. Meaning is known. The definition, format and meaning of every field are explicit, so it is easy to query.

    Real-life examples

    ExamplesUniversity: Student(roll_no, name, branch, marks, attendance).
    Bank: Transaction(txn_id, acc_no, amount, date).
    Railway: IRCTC PNR record (PNR, train, date, class, berth, status).
    Retail: Sales(bill_no, item, qty, price).
    Hospital: Patient(patient_id, age, blood_group).

    Why is structured data "easy"? (5 quick points)

    Memory hook: D-S-I-S-A, "Do Someone's Interesting Stories Again": DML (insert, update, delete are simple) · Security (encryption, access control) · Indexing (fast search) · Scalability (partition and replicate) · ACID transactions (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability).

    Sources: databases (Oracle, MySQL, SQL Server), spreadsheets, OLTP systems (banking, POS, reservations), sensors that write fixed-format readings, web forms.

    Limitation: the structure must be decided in advance and changing it is costly. Reviews, photos and videos simply do not fit, and that is the other 80–90% of all data.

    New words
    Schema
    The fixed design of a table: column names and types
    Primary key
    A column whose value is unique in every row
    Foreign key
    A column that points to the primary key of another table
    ACID
    Four promises that keep a transaction safe: Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability

    § 2.4Semi-structured Data, OEM & XML 2023 Q4(b)

    I · Definition
    Professor's definitionSemi-structured data does not conform to a formal data model and cannot be stored in rows and columns as in a database. It does, however, have tags and markers that help group the data and describe how it is stored, giving some metadata, though not enough for full management and automation. Similar entities are grouped and organised in a hierarchy, and the attributes within a group may or may not be the same.
    II · Characteristics & Sources
    • Inconsistent structure: records of the same type can have different fields (one customer has 2 phones, another has none).
    • Self-describing: label/value pairs; the schema is blended with the data.
    • Hierarchical or nested (tree or graph).
    • Data objects may have different attributes, not known in advance.

    Sources: XML (web services, SOAP), JSON (REST APIs, MongoDB, Couchbase), email (structured headers + unstructured body), HTML, TCP/IP packets, zipped files, binary executables, integration of data from heterogeneous sources, NoSQL stores, log files, EDI messages.

    III · Managing, Modelling (OEM), Storing & Extracting

    Managing semi-structured data (professor's three approaches):

    1. Schemas: describe the structure and constraints (XML Schema/XSD, DTD, JSON Schema). The problem is that requirements change constantly, and changes in data force changes in the schema.
    2. Graph-based data models: a "schema-less" or self-describing approach. Relationships and hierarchies are a tree-like graph, where vertices contain objects or entities and leaves contain data. OEM is the classic example.
    3. XML: widely used to store and exchange semi-structured data; user-defined tags store data in hierarchical or nested form; the schema is not tightly coupled to the data.
    OEM: the Object Exchange Model

    OEM was proposed in Stanford's TSIMMIS project (Papakonstantinou, Garcia-Molina & Widom, 1995) to exchange data between heterogeneous sources. Data is a labelled directed graph. Each object has an object ID (OID), a label (what it is, e.g. "name"), a type, and a value. Atomic objects hold a value (string, integer); complex objects hold a set of references to sub-objects. There is no schema: labels travel with the data.

    Challenges in storing semi-structured data: storage cost (tags repeat with every record); an RDBMS cannot handle it directly because the structure is irregular; irregular and partial structure (some fields missing); implicit structure (the schema is not declared); evolving schemas; the distinction between schema and data is blurred (a label is both); queries become less efficient.

    Possible solutions: special-purpose DBMSs (XML-native databases such as BaseX, MarkLogic; document stores such as MongoDB); mapping to an RDBMS (shredding XML into tables, or JSON columns in PostgreSQL); OEM/graph storage; XML with schemas where possible.

    Extracting information: challenges: data is often in flat files, comes from heterogeneous sources, and is incomplete or irrelevant. Solutions: indexing (XML indexes), OEM-based querying (Lorel), XML query languages (XPath, XQuery, XSLT), mining tools.

    IV · XML as a Solution, and Converting XML to Structured Form (PYQ 2023 Q4b)

    XML (eXtensible Markup Language, W3C, 1998) lets users define their own tags, nests data hierarchically, is self-describing, platform-independent and human-readable, and can be validated with a DTD or XSD. This makes it a standard for exchanging semi-structured data between heterogeneous systems (the hospital's HL7 messages, GST e-invoices, RSS). Yes, XML can be converted into structured format by mapping elements to tables, repeated elements to rows, and child elements or attributes to columns ("shredding"):

    <students> <student roll="2201"> <name>Asha</name> <branch>CSE</branch> <phone>98xxxx1111</phone> </student> <student roll="2202"> <name>Ravi</name> <branch>IT</branch> <email>r@geu.ac.in</email> </student> </students> ↓ shred (XPath /students/student) STUDENT( roll PK | name | branch | phone | email ) 2201 | Asha | CSE | 98xxxx1111 | NULL 2202 | Ravi | IT | NULL | r@geu.ac.in

    Steps: (1) study or infer the XML schema (XSD); (2) map each repeating element to a table and each leaf or attribute to a column, choosing a key; (3) flatten nested repeating groups into child tables with foreign keys (e.g. a student with many courses gives a STUDENT_COURSE table); (4) handle missing elements as NULLs; (5) load with tools (SQL Server OPENXML, Oracle XMLTABLE, Python pandas.read_xml, ETL tools). Caveats: deep or irregular nesting produces many sparse columns or tables, and some hierarchy semantics are lost, so an XML-native database may be preferable when the structure changes often.

    V · Critical Evaluation

    JSON has largely replaced XML for web APIs (lighter, native to JavaScript), but XML still dominates in documents, enterprise integration and regulated data exchange. Semi-structured data is the bridge in BI: much of what enters a data warehouse through ETL arrives as XML or JSON from APIs and must be parsed and conformed to the dimensional model (Unit 3).

    § 2.5Unstructured data in practice: UIMA, best practices, backup and web pages 2023 Q3(b), Q4(a), Q5(a)

    UIMA: Unstructured Information Management Architecture
    UIMAAn open, industry-standard architecture and framework (developed by IBM, now Apache UIMA, standardised by OASIS in 2009) for building applications that analyse large volumes of unstructured content (text, audio, video) to discover knowledge relevant to an end user. It famously powered parts of IBM Watson, which won Jeopardy! in 2011.

    Why UIMA matters: it separates analysis components (reusable, pluggable annotators written by different teams) from the framework that runs them (scaled out across machines), and it turns unstructured text into structured annotations that BI and search can use.

    IV · Impact of Unstructured Data on Backup & Recovery (PYQ 2023 Q5a)
    • Volume and growth: backup windows overflow because TB–PB of files grow 40–60% a year, and full backups take too long.
    • Many small files and deep folder trees: file-by-file scanning is slow (millions of emails, images); the metadata overhead dominates.
    • Duplication: the same attachment is stored many times, inflating backup size. The solution is deduplication.
    • Cost: storage, network bandwidth, cloud egress and retention costs rise.
    • Recovery time and point objectives (RTO/RPO): restoring huge unindexed file shares is slow, and finding one needed file ("the contract from 2021") is hard without metadata.
    • Consistency: files open during backup; no transactional guarantees as in a DB.
    • Compliance and security: unstructured data holds hidden sensitive information (PII in PDFs), so backups must be encrypted, retained or deleted by policy (DPDP Act, legal hold); "dark data" is backed up without anyone knowing what it is.
    • Ransomware targets file shares, so immutable, air-gapped copies are needed.

    Responses: incremental-forever backup and snapshots, deduplication and compression, tiering (hot/cold/archive), classification before backup, CAS or WORM for compliance archives, cloud object storage with versioning, and the 3-2-1 rule (3 copies, 2 media, 1 off-site).

    IV-b · Best Practices for Managing the Growth of Unstructured Data (PYQ 2023 Q3b)
    1. Discover and classify what exists: data inventory, auto-classification by type, owner, sensitivity and value.
    2. Apply metadata and tagging at creation, with naming conventions and document templates.
    3. Define information-lifecycle and retention policies: keep, archive or delete by age, value and legal need.
    4. Tiered storage: move cold data to cheaper storage or the cloud; use object stores for scale.
    5. Deduplicate and compress; remove ROT data (Redundant, Obsolete, Trivial).
    6. Centralise in content-management systems (SharePoint, Documentum) or data lakes rather than scattered drives.
    7. Index and enable enterprise search; apply text mining, NLP and UIMA to extract structure.
    8. Govern and secure: access controls, encryption, DLP, audit; comply with privacy law.
    9. Monitor growth with storage analytics and quotas; budget capacity.
    10. Convert where possible: OCR scans, transcribe audio, extract data from forms into structured stores.
    V · Web Pages: Unstructured Despite HTML (PYQ 2023 Q4a)

    HTML does have rich structure, but it is structure of presentation, not meaning. Tags such as <div>, <p>, <h1>, <table> say how to display content, not what it is. A price and a phone number both sit in plain <span>s. So:

    • The content inside the tags is free text, images and media with no data model, and its meaning must be interpreted.
    • There is no fixed schema across pages: two product pages from different sites (or the same site after a redesign) arrange the same information differently.
    • HTML is often not well-formed (browsers tolerate missing closing tags), and content is generated dynamically by JavaScript.
    • You cannot run SQL-like queries such as "all laptops under ₹50,000" across web pages. You need scraping, wrappers or NLP.

    So web pages are usually treated as unstructured (some texts say semi-structured, since the markup gives partial structure). Semantic efforts such as schema.org microdata / JSON-LD and RDF add meaning-level tags precisely to fix this gap.

    § 3.1What is OLTP? Purpose and two real examples Assignment 2 · Q1

    The big ideaOLTP (On-Line Transaction Processing) runs the day-to-day business. It handles thousands of tiny, quick, safe operations (record a sale, book a seat, withdraw cash) on current data, and it never loses or garbles a transaction.

    Definition to write

    OLTPA class of systems that manage transaction-oriented applications: a large number of short, concurrent, atomic operations (INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE, simple SELECT) on current, detailed data, with fast response and strict integrity (ACID).

    Purpose

    To capture and update operational data reliably, in real time, for many users at once. It answers "what is happening right now?" and never "what happened over the last five years?".

    Two examples (write both, with steps)

    Example 1: ATM / core bankingYou withdraw ₹2,000. The system checks your balance, debits your account, logs the transaction and gives you cash. If any step fails, all steps are undone (atomicity). Thousands of customers do this at once without seeing each other's data (isolation).
    Example 2: IRCTC railway reservationYou book berth 32 on the 12:30 train. The system must check it is free, mark it taken and issue a PNR, while thousands of people compete for the same seat. Two people must never get the same berth.

    More: supermarket billing (POS), online order placement, college fee payment and course registration, hotel booking.

    Queries OLTP handles easily vs queries it cannot (asked in 2023)

    -- EASY: point lookups and small updates on current data, in milliseconds SELECT balance FROM account WHERE acc_no = 'SB1029'; UPDATE account SET balance = balance - 2000 WHERE acc_no = 'SB1029'; INSERT INTO orders(order_id, cust_id, item, qty) VALUES (…); SELECT status FROM pnr WHERE pnr_no = '4521789632'; SELECT * FROM student WHERE roll_no = 2201;

    Cannot answer well (analytical, historical, many-dimensional): "Total sales by region by quarter for the last 5 years, and which category grew fastest?" · "Compare this Diwali's sales with the last three Diwalis, city by city." · "Marks trend per subject across 10 batches."

    Why not? OLTP keeps only current data (history is overwritten), it is normalised so such queries need many joins, data sits in separate systems, and giant scans would slow down live transactions at the counter.

    § 3.8Ralph Kimball's approach: why bottom-up? Advantages and limitations Assignment 3 · Q2

    Because the enterprise data warehouse is assembled from the bottom (individual, subject-specific data marts) up to the whole.     1. Choose one business process (for example retail sales).
    2. Decide the grain (one row = one item on one bill).
    3. Identify the dimensions (date, product, store, customer).
    4. Identify the facts (units, amount).
    5. Build the star schema mart and load it with ETL; hand it to users.
    6. Repeat for the next process, reusing the conformed dimensions.

    § 3.9What is data integration? How do we get a unified view from heterogeneous sources? Assignment 3 · Q3

    The big ideaThe same real-world thing (a patient, a customer) is described differently in every system. Data integration combines data from different sources into one unified view, keeping it accurate and reliable. It has two jobs: make the structures agree (schema integration) and match up the records that mean the same thing (instance integration).

    Definition to write

    Data integrationCombining data residing in different sources to provide users with a unified view of it: the ability to consolidate data from several sources while maintaining its integrity and reliability.

    Why is it hard? (heterogeneity)

    Sources differ in format (tables, XML, spreadsheets), names (cust_name vs customerName), data types and units (kg vs lb, DD/MM vs MM/DD), codes (M/F vs 1/0), and quality (missing values, typos, duplicates).

    Two levels of integration

    Schema integrationInstance integration
    What it doesUnifies the structure. Sources describe the same entity type differently; mapping rules resolve the differences so users see one uniform source.Unifies the records. Identifies all instances that represent the same real-world entity (entity resolution, de-duplication).
    Examplecust_name (CRM) and customerName (billing) both mapped to DIM_CUSTOMER.name; date formats standardised"R. Sharma, Dehradun" in the lab system and "Rahul Sharma, DDN" in pharmacy recognised as one patient

    How is the unified view produced? (five steps)

    1. Identify the sources and study their structures and meanings.
    2. Extract the data from each source.
    3. Clean it: fix errors, missing values and duplicates.
    4. Transform it: map to a common schema; standardise names, codes, units and formats; match records for the same entity.
    5. Load it into the warehouse, where users query one consistent copy (through OLAP, reports, dashboards).

    Steps 2–5 are exactly ETL, explained in Q4.

    The other way: a federated (virtual) database

    Instead of copying data, a federated database system keeps the databases autonomous and puts a virtual layer on top. A query is translated and sent to each source at run time. Advantage: always fresh data, no copying. Disadvantage: slower, and no long history like a warehouse. It is a feasible alternative to physically merging the sources.

    Mapping rules
    Rules saying "this field here equals that field there"
    Entity resolution
    Working out that two records describe the same real-world thing
    Federated database
    A virtual database over several autonomous databases; data stays where it is

    § 3.17Four data-mining techniques: classification, clustering, association rules, prediction Assignment 4 · Q6

    The big ideaTwo questions decide the technique. Do we already know the groups? Yes → classification (put an item into a known box). No → clustering (let the data form its own groups). Are we looking at things bought or done together? → association rules. Do we want a future number? → prediction.

    Memory hook: Classify = sorting letters into labelled pigeonholes. Cluster = sorting a pile of unlabelled photos into piles that look alike. Associate = "people who buy this also buy that". Predict = weather forecast for a number.

    Association rules: the two numbers to know

    Worked exampleOf 100 bills, 20 contain bread, 12 contain butter and 10 contain both.
    Rule: bread → butter
    Support = how often both appear together = 10 / 100 = 10%.
    Confidence = of bills with bread, how many also have butter = 10 / 20 = 50%.
    A rule is interesting only if both pass a minimum threshold.

    Classification vs prediction: the confusing pair

    Classification predicts a category (yes/no, spam/not spam, low/medium/high risk). Prediction (regression) predicts a number (₹ sales, marks). Some books call both "prediction"; in your answer say clearly which you mean.

    Supervised learning
    Learning from examples whose answer (label) is known
    Unsupervised learning
    Finding structure in data with no labels
    Support
    How frequently an itemset appears in all transactions
    Confidence
    How often the rule is right: P(B given A)
    Regression
    Fitting a line or curve to predict a number

    Flashcards · Unit Three

    Includes cumulative cards from Units One and Two (↺).

    OLTP: definition and two examples.
    Many short, concurrent, ACID operations on current detailed data. ATM/core banking; IRCTC ticket booking (also POS billing).
    Why can OLTP not answer "sales by region by quarter for 5 years"?
    It holds only current data, is normalised (many joins), is spread over separate systems, and big scans would slow live transactions.
    OLTP vs ERP in one line?
    OLTP is a processing style; ERP is an integrated business software suite that runs on OLTP. All ERPs are OLTP; not all OLTP is ERP.
    Five quick differences: OLTP vs OLAP.
    Run vs analyse · current vs historical · read/write vs read-mostly · normalised vs star/snowflake · milliseconds vs seconds-minutes (GB vs TB-PB).
    1-D, 2-D, 3-D, hypercube?
    List → table → cube (product × region × time) → hypercube (add more dimensions).
    MOLAP vs ROLAP vs HOLAP?
    MOLAP: pre-built cube, fast, limited size. ROLAP: relational tables + SQL, big, slower. HOLAP: detail in RDBMS, aggregates in a cube, drill-through.
    Star vs snowflake vs constellation?
    Star: one fact + flat dimensions (fast). Snowflake: dimensions split into sub-tables (less redundancy, more joins). Constellation: several facts sharing dimensions (drill-across).
    Fact table vs dimension table?
    Fact: measures + foreign keys, long and narrow. Dimension: descriptive attributes and hierarchies, short and wide.
    Slice vs dice?
    Slice fixes one dimension (2-D result). Dice fixes two or more (a sub-cube).
    Roll-up, drill-down, pivot, drill-across?
    Roll-up: go up a hierarchy. Drill-down: go down. Pivot: rotate axes. Drill-across: query several fact tables via shared dimensions.
    Design the healthcare OLAP: fact and dimensions?
    Fact: treatment (cost, length of stay, outcome). Dimensions: patient, doctor, disease, treatment, department, date.
    Which data type goes into a star schema directly?
    Structured data. Semi-structured is parsed by ETL first; unstructured must be mined for facts (sentiment, entities) before it can be stored.
    Inmon's definition of a data warehouse?
    A subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant, non-volatile collection of data in support of management's decision-making. (S-I-T-N)
    Explain the four characteristics in one line each.
    Subject-oriented: organised by business subject. Integrated: unified formats and codes. Time-variant: keeps history. Non-volatile: not updated or deleted in place.
    Six goals of a data warehouse?
    Accessibility, credibility, flexibility to change, fact-based decisions, security, consistency.
    Why is Kimball's approach called bottom-up?
    The warehouse is built up from individual subject-area data marts joined by conformed dimensions ("a DW is all the data marts in an enterprise").
    Kimball advantages and limitations?
    Advantages: fast, cheap, business-friendly, iterative, fast queries. Limitations: single version of truth can fragment across marts; needs discipline to conform dimensions; redundancy.
    Inmon vs Kimball in one line?
    Inmon: top-down, enterprise 3NF warehouse first, marts derived from it. Kimball: bottom-up, dimensional marts first, combined into the warehouse.
    Define data integration.
    Combining data from different sources into a unified view while maintaining integrity and reliability.
    Schema integration vs instance integration?
    Schema: mapping rules unify differently structured descriptions of the same entity type. Instance: match records that are the same real-world entity (entity resolution).
    Role of E, C, T, L?
    Extract: pull from sources into staging. Clean: fix errors, nulls, duplicates. Transform: standardise, derive, conform keys. Load: insert into fact and dimension tables.
    Bank case: four needs a warehouse meets?
    Integration (360° customer view), historical data (trends, risk), consistency (one set of numbers), security (role-based access, masking).
    Retail case: consistency, accessibility, credibility?
    Consistency: conformed dimensions and master data. Accessibility: star schema plus BI tools. Credibility: data-quality rules, metadata and lineage.
    What is a federated database?
    A virtual database over autonomous source databases; queries go to the sources at run time; no copying but slower and no history.
    Where does OLAP sit relative to the warehouse?
    The warehouse stores the integrated data; the OLAP server on top presents it as cubes for slice, dice, drill and pivot.
    Define a data mart. Data mart vs data warehouse?
    A subject/department-specific subset of a warehouse. Mart: one subject, GBs, weeks to build, one user group. Warehouse: enterprise-wide, many subjects, TBs, months to years.
    Dependent vs independent data mart?
    Dependent: fed from the enterprise warehouse (consistent). Independent: fed directly from sources (quick but may create inconsistent islands).
    What is metadata?
    Data about data: what data is, where it came from, and what it means.
    Technical vs business metadata, with examples.
    Technical: table/column names, types, mappings, ETL schedules, lineage. Business: definitions ("active customer"), KPI formulas, data owners, report descriptions.
    Five reasons metadata is important?
    Finding and understanding data, trust (lineage), drives ETL mappings, impact analysis, consistent definitions and governance.
    Student-performance warehouse: fact and dimensions?
    Fact: result (marks, grade point, attendance %, pass flag). Dimensions: student, course, department, exam, academic year.
    Define data mining. Why is it important in BI?
    Discovering hidden patterns and predictions in large data (KDD). It moves BI from what happened to what will happen, and finds patterns no one thought to query.
    Data mining vs data warehousing?
    Warehousing stores and organises integrated historical data; mining analyses it to discover patterns. The warehouse supplies clean data; mining supplies insight.
    Classification vs clustering?
    Classification: predefined classes, supervised (loan default yes/no). Clustering: groups discovered from data, unsupervised (customer segments).
    Association rule: support and confidence?
    Support = share of all transactions with both A and B. Confidence = share of transactions with A that also have B. Example: bread → butter.
    Prediction vs classification?
    Prediction estimates a number (sales, marks) using regression or time-series. Classification predicts a category.
    Why must ETL and cleaning come before data mining?
    Mining finds patterns in whatever it is given, so dirty or inconsistent data produces false patterns. The warehouse supplies clean, integrated data.
    Connect BI with Baldrige.
    Baldrige category 4 (Measurement, Analysis and Knowledge Management) and the value "management by fact" are what a warehouse, OLAP and dashboards deliver.
