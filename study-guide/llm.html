    Unit Zero · Before We Begin

    Machine Learning from Zero

    No maths or coding background needed. Everything the LLM units rely on, built up one small step at a time.

    Not a syllabus unit by itself, but every idea here (parameters, training, loss, neurons, probabilities, vectors, GPUs) appears in Units 1 and 2 and in exam answers. Read it once slowly. It takes about 40 minutes.

    Step 0.1What does it mean for a computer to "learn"?

    A computer is very fast and very obedient, but it has no common sense. On its own it does exactly what it is told, nothing more.

    The old way: rules written by a person. Suppose you want a program that says whether an email is spam. The old way is to sit down and write rules: "if the email contains the word 'lottery', call it spam", "if it says 'you have won ₹10 lakh', call it spam". This works a little, but spammers change their wording, you need thousands of rules, and you will always miss some.

    The new way: learning from examples. Instead of writing rules, you collect examples: 10,000 emails that people have already marked "spam" or "not spam". You give these to the computer and let it work out the rules by itself: which words, patterns and senders usually mean spam. That is machine learning (ML): the computer finds the rules from examples instead of a person writing them.

    A child learns what a "dog" is by seeing many dogs, not by being given a definition. Machine learning works the same way: show enough examples and the computer picks up the pattern.

    A Large Language Model (LLM) like ChatGPT is a machine-learning model that learned the patterns of human language by reading an enormous amount of text.

    Step 0.2Computers only understand numbers

    Deep inside, a computer can only store and calculate with numbers. It has no idea what the word "cat" means. So anything we want a computer to learn from (words, pictures, sounds) must first be turned into numbers.

    • A photo is already numbers: each tiny dot (pixel) is stored as how red, green and blue it is, e.g. (255, 200, 0).
    • A letter has a code number: in the ASCII code, "A" = 65, "B" = 66.
    • A word can be given an ID number from a dictionary: "cat" = 3797, "sat" = 3332. LLMs do exactly this. The pieces of text they number are called tokens (Unit 1 explains them).

    An ID number alone doesn't carry meaning (3797 is not "closer" to "kitten" than to "car"). Step 0.7 shows the smarter trick LLMs use: turning each word into a list of numbers that does carry meaning.

    Step 0.3A model is a machine with knobs

    Think of a model as a box. Numbers go in, the box does some arithmetic, and a number (the prediction) comes out. Inside the box are knobs that control the arithmetic. Turning the knobs changes the answers.

    The simplest possible model. A fruit seller charges by weight. We want to predict the price from the weight:

    price = w × weight w is the KNOB (the price per kg). If w = 100, then 2 kg → ₹200.

    This model has one knob. Knobs are officially called parameters or weights, and those words appear everywhere in the LLM units. A slightly bigger model might be price = w × weight + b. The extra knob b (called the bias) is a fixed amount added every time, like a ₹10 carry-bag charge.

    The key point: a model is its knob settings. "Training a model" just means finding good knob settings. When people say "GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters", they mean it has 175 billion knobs, all of which were set automatically by learning.

    Step 0.4How the knobs get set: guess, check, adjust

    No person turns 175 billion knobs by hand. The computer sets them with a simple loop, repeated many times:

    1. Guess: use the current knob settings to make a prediction.
    2. Check: compare it with the correct answer. How wrong was it? This "wrongness score" is called the loss (or error). A bigger loss means a worse guess.
    3. Adjust: nudge each knob a little in the direction that would have made the guess less wrong.

    Worked example. We know 2 kg of mangoes costs ₹200. We don't know the knob w, so we start with a bad guess, w = 50. Rule for adjusting: w goes up by 0.1 × error × weight.

    Nobody told the computer "the answer is 100". It found it by guessing, checking and adjusting. The loss shrinks every round. This loop is all that "training" means, whether the model has 1 knob or 175 billion.

    The hill-in-the-fog picture (gradient descent)

    Imagine you are standing on a hilly field in thick fog and you want to reach the lowest point. You can't see far, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, and take another step. Eventually you reach the bottom.

    • Gradient = the slope under your feet: which direction is downhill for each knob, and how steep.
    • Gradient descent = repeatedly stepping downhill. This is how every LLM is trained.
    • Learning rate = the size of each step (0.1 in our mango rule). Too big and you jump over the valley; too small and you take forever.
    • Backpropagation = the bookkeeping trick that works out the slope for all the knobs at once, working backwards from the error.

    Step 0.5Neurons and neural networks

    The price formula was too simple for language. We need a model that can learn very complicated patterns. The building block is a neuron. It is loosely inspired by brain cells, but really it is just a tiny calculator that weighs up evidence and decides.

    Example: should I go to the cricket match? Three pieces of evidence, each 1 (yes) or 0 (no), each with a weight showing how much it matters:

    Weather is good? 1 × weight +2 = +2 Friend is going? 1 × weight +3 = +3 Exam tomorrow? 1 × weight −6 = −6 total = −1 → below 0 → switch OFF → "Don't go" If there's no exam: 2 + 3 + 0 = +5 → above 0 → switch ON → "Go!"

    That is all a neuron does: multiply each input by its weight, add them up, and pass the total through a switch (called the activation function) that decides how strongly to "fire". The weights are knobs, learned by the guess-check-adjust loop.

    Why stack them? One neuron can only make a simple yes/no judgement. The first layer spots simple things, the next layer combines those into more complex things, and so on. In an image model: edges → shapes → eyes and ears → "cat". An LLM is a very deep neural network (GPT-3 has 96 big layers) of a special design called the Transformer (Unit 1, § 1.3).

    Step 0.6Scores into percentages: probability and softmax

    An LLM never "knows" the next word for sure. It gives every possible next word a score, and then turns the scores into probabilities: percentages that add up to 100%.

    The turning-into-percentages step is called softmax. It does two things: (1) makes every score positive, using the special number e ≈ 2.718 raised to the power of the score; (2) divides each by the total so they add up to 100%.

    Sentence so far: "I have a pet ___" Raw scores: cat 2, dog 1, car 0 e^2 = 7.39 e^1 = 2.72 e^0 = 1.00 total = 11.11 cat = 7.39/11.11 = 66.5% dog = 2.72/11.11 = 24.5% car = 1.00/11.11 = 9.0% (adds to 100%)

    Notice that softmax exaggerates the gaps: a score just 1 point higher becomes almost 3× more likely. The model then picks the next word from these percentages (Unit 1 calls this decoding).

    Step 0.7Words as points on a map: vectors and embeddings

    Back to the problem from Step 0.2: an ID number such as "cat = 3797" carries no meaning. The fix is to describe each word with a list of numbers, like coordinates on a map.

    Imagine a map with two directions: "how much is it an animal?" (up) and "how big is it?" (right). Every word gets a location:

    • A list of numbers like (1.5, 8.5) is called a vector. It is simply an arrow pointing to a spot on the map.
    • The vector that represents a word is called its embedding. Nobody chooses these coordinates by hand: they are knobs, learned during training.
    • Because similar words sit close together, the computer can now measure meaning: "cat" is near "kitten" and far from "bus".
    • The dot product of two vectors measures how much two arrows point the same way: multiply matching numbers and add. (1, 2) · (3, 1) = 1×3 + 2×1 = 5. A big positive number means "similar direction", near zero means "unrelated". Attention in the Transformer (§ 1.3) is built on dot products.

    Step 0.8Matrices and GPUs: doing millions of sums at once

    Look back at the neuron: multiply inputs by weights and add them up. A layer has thousands of neurons all doing this to the same inputs at the same time. Writing all their weights in a table (rows × columns) gives a matrix, and doing all those weighted sums in one go is called matrix multiplication. That is the main operation inside an LLM, repeated billions of times.

    3 neurons, each looking at the same 2 inputs (x₁ = 2, x₂ = 3): weights table (matrix) inputs outputs neuron A: [ 1 0 ] 1×2 + 0×3 = 2 neuron B: [ 2 1 ] × [2, 3] = 2×2 + 1×3 = 7 neuron C: [ 0 −1 ] 0×2 − 1×3 = −3

    Why GPUs? A normal processor (CPU) is like a few brilliant professors: they can do anything, but only a few things at a time. A GPU (graphics card, originally built for video games) is like thousands of school students who can each only do simple multiplication, all working at the same moment. Matrix multiplication is millions of simple multiply-and-add jobs that don't depend on each other, which is exactly the work thousands of students can split up. That is why AI runs on GPUs (and Google's similar chips called TPUs).

    Step 0.9A few more ideas you'll meet

    • Training vs inference. Training is the long, expensive learning phase (setting the knobs: weeks of GPU time). Inference is using the finished model (you typing a question and getting an answer). The knobs no longer change during inference.
    • Supervised learning. Learning from examples that come with correct answers attached by people (emails labelled spam or not spam).
    • Self-supervised learning. The data provides its own answers. Take any sentence, hide the last word, and ask the model to guess it. The real text is the answer key. This is why LLMs can learn from the whole internet without anyone labelling anything.
    • Overfitting. A student who memorises last year's answers word for word, then fails when the questions change slightly. A model can do the same: perfect on its training examples, poor on new ones. We want understanding of patterns, not memorisation.
    • Dataset, epoch, batch. The dataset is all the examples. A batch is a small handful processed together in one guess-check-adjust round. An epoch is one full pass through the whole dataset.

    Step 0.10Putting it together: what an LLM is

    You now have every piece. Here is a whole LLM in plain words:

    1. Take a huge amount of text from the internet, books and code. Chop it into small pieces called tokens and give each an ID number (Step 0.2).
    2. Turn each token into a list of numbers, its embedding, a location on a meaning map (Step 0.7).
    3. Pass these through a very deep neural network (Step 0.5) of a special design called the Transformer, which lets every word look at every other word to work out the context.
    4. At the end, give every possible next token a score, and turn the scores into percentages with softmax (Step 0.6).
    5. Training: hide the next word in real text, let the model guess, measure how wrong it was (loss), and nudge all the billions of knobs a little downhill (gradient descent, Step 0.4). Repeat trillions of times on thousands of GPUs (Step 0.8).
    6. Using it: give it your question; it predicts one word, adds it on, predicts the next, and so on until the answer is complete.

    An LLM is the world's most thoroughly trained "guess the next word" player. It has practised on so much text that, to predict the next word well, it had to absorb grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and writing styles along the way.

    How an LLM Is Built

    What an LLM is, how we got here, what's inside it, and what it takes to train one.

    Syllabus: overview of LLMs · evolution from early models · transformer architecture · attention mechanisms · data & preprocessing · optimisation & training challenges · hardware & computation

    § 1.1What Is a Large Language Model?

    The big ideaAn LLM is a computer program that has read an enormous amount of text and learned one skill: guessing the next word. Repeat that skill over and over and it can write essays, answer questions, translate and chat.

    What is a "language model"?

    You already use a tiny one. When you type on your phone, the keyboard suggests the next word. Type "Happy" and it suggests "birthday".

    A language model does exactly this. You give it some words, and it tells you which word is most likely to come next, and how likely each option is.

    Why is it called "large"?

    Because it is huge in three ways at once:

    • Huge number of "knobs". Inside the model are adjustable numbers called parameters. GPT-3 has 175 billion of them. (Unit 0, Step 0.3 explains knobs.)
    • Huge amount of reading. GPT-3 learned from about 300 billion tokens of text: websites, books, code and papers.
    • Huge amount of computing. Training needed thousands of powerful chips running for weeks. For GPT-3 that was about 3,640 petaflop/s-days of work.

    What are "tokens"?

    A model does not read whole words. It reads small pieces of words called tokens, like Lego bricks.

    Example"Unbelievable!" is read as four tokens: Un + believ + able + !
    Each token has an ID number, because computers only work with numbers.

    Two useful facts:

    • Roughly 1 token ≈ ¾ of a word. So 1,000 tokens ≈ 750 words.
    • A model can only look at a limited number of tokens at once. This limit is called the context window (for example 4,000 or 128,000 tokens).

    How does guessing one word turn into a whole answer?

    It repeats the guess in a loop:

    1. Read all the words so far.
    2. Give every possible next word a probability.
    3. Pick one word.
    4. Stick it on the end, and go back to step 1.

    This loop is called autoregressive generation ("auto" = self, "regressive" = using its own previous output). A full paragraph is just this loop run hundreds of times.

    How does it pick the next word? (Decoding)

    Once the model has its list of probabilities, it still has to choose. The method of choosing is called decoding.

    MethodWhat it does, simplyResult
    GreedyAlways pick the top wordSafe but boring and repetitive
    Beam searchKeep the best few sentences going at once, choose the best at the endGood for translation
    Top-kPick randomly, but only from the top k words (e.g. top 40)A bit of variety
    Top-p (nucleus)Pick randomly from the smallest group of words that together make up p% (e.g. 90%)The usual choice for chat

    Temperature is a dial for how adventurous the choice is. Low temperature means "play it safe" (predictable answers). High temperature means "take risks" (creative, but more mistakes).

    What can LLMs do?

    One model can write, summarise, translate, answer questions, reason step by step and hold a conversation. Some skills, like multi-step reasoning, only appear once models get big enough. These are called emergent abilities: you can't predict them by testing a small model.

    Well-known LLMs include GPT-4 (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google), LLaMA (Meta) and Mistral. The names change often, but they are all built on the same design, the Transformer (§ 1.3).

    What's the catch?

    An LLM is trained to produce text that sounds likely, not text that is checked to be true. So it can state false things confidently (this is called hallucination, Unit 2). It also picks up biases from what it read, and it doesn't know anything that happened after its training ended.

    § 1.2How We Got Here: From Counting Words to ChatGPT

    The big ideaComputers have been trying to "guess the next word" for decades. Each new method fixed the biggest weakness of the one before. The big turning point was the Transformer in 2017, and every modern LLM is built on it.

    Stage 1 (1990s): counting words, called "n-grams"

    The simplest method is to count. Read lots of text and keep a tally: after "the cat", which word came next, and how often?

    ExampleAfter "the cat", the tally might say: "sat" 40 times, "ran" 25 times, "is" 20 times. So the guess is "sat".
    An n-gram looks at the last few words only. A trigram (n = 3) uses the last 2 words to guess the 3rd.

    Why it failed:

    • Short memory. It only sees the last 2–3 words, so it forgets the start of the sentence.
    • Most word combinations were never seen. If "the purple cat" never appeared in the text, it gets zero chance. This is called sparsity.
    • No meaning. To it, "cat" and "kitten" are just different spellings, completely unrelated.

    Stage 2 (2013): giving words a meaning, called "word embeddings"

    The fix for "no meaning" was to give every word a position on a map, written as a list of numbers. Words used in similar ways end up close together (Unit 0, Step 0.7).

    Example"cat" sits near "kitten" and "dog", far from "bus".
    The map even captures relationships: king − man + woman ≈ queen. The "male to female" step is the same direction on the map.

    The famous methods are word2vec (Google, 2013) and GloVe.

    Why it wasn't enough: every word gets one fixed spot. So "bank" (of a river) and "bank" (for money) share the same spot, even though they mean different things.

    Stage 3 (2010s): reading in order with a memory, called "RNN" and "LSTM"

    Next, models learned to read a sentence one word at a time, keeping a running summary like a sticky note that gets updated after every word. That summary is called the hidden state.

    These models are called RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks). Improved versions called LSTM and GRU have "gates" that decide what to remember and what to forget, so they hold on to information longer.

    Why they weren't enough:

    • Slow. You can't read word 50 until you've finished word 49, so the work can't be split across many chips at once.
    • Still forgetful. On long passages, the sticky note loses the beginning.

    Stage 4 (2014): letting the model look back, called "attention"

    For translation, early models squeezed a whole sentence into one summary before translating it. Long sentences lost detail, like summarising a whole page on one sticky note.

    Attention fixed this. While writing each word, the model is allowed to look back at every word of the original and focus on the ones that matter right now, like using a highlighter.

    Stage 5 (2017): the breakthrough, called the "Transformer"

    A Google paper called "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) made a bold move: throw away the one-word-at-a-time reading completely and use only attention.

    • Every word can look at every other word directly, so nothing is forgotten.
    • All words are processed at the same time, so training is much faster on GPUs.
    • It keeps getting better the bigger you make it.

    This design, the Transformer, is inside every modern LLM.

    Stage 6 (2018 onward): read everything first, then use it, called "pretraining"

    Two families of Transformer models appeared in 2018:

    BERT (Google)GPT (OpenAI)
    How it readsLooks at the words on both sides at onceReads left to right only
    How it's trainedFill in hidden (masked) words in the middle of sentencesGuess the next word
    Good atUnderstanding: sorting, searching, tagging textWriting: essays, chat, code
    Part of the Transformer usedEncoder onlyDecoder only

    Then models grew bigger and bigger: GPT-1 (117 million parameters, 2018), GPT-2 (1.5 billion, 2019), GPT-3 (175 billion, 2020). GPT-3 could learn a new task just from a few examples written in the question (few-shot learning). Finally, extra training with human feedback (RLHF) taught models to follow instructions politely, and that produced ChatGPT in November 2022.

    § 1.3Inside the Transformer: How Attention Works

    The big ideaA Transformer turns each word into numbers, then lets every word look at every other word to work out what it means in this sentence. This "looking" is called attention. The model repeats this many times, then guesses the next word.

    The journey of a sentence through the Transformer

    1. Chop the text into tokens.
    2. Turn each token into a list of numbers (its embedding) and add its position number.
    3. Attention: every word looks at the other words and updates its meaning.
    4. Think: each word goes through a small neural network on its own.
    5. Repeat steps 3–4 many times (GPT-3 does it 96 times). Each round is called a layer or block.
    6. Score every possible next word and turn the scores into percentages (softmax).

    Step 2: why add "position numbers"?

    Attention looks at all words at once, so on its own it has no idea about word order. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would look the same.

    So each word gets its position mixed into its numbers, like a seat number. This is called positional encoding.

    How it's done in the original paperPosition is written using wave patterns (sine and cosine), a different wave for each number in the list:
    PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d)) and PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d))
    You don't need to understand the waves. Just know that input = word's numbers + position's numbers.

    Step 3: attention, explained with a classroom

    Take the sentence: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." What does "it" mean? A person knows "it" = the animal. Here is how the model works that out.

    Picture each word as a student in a class. Every student gets three things:

    NameIn the classroomFor the word "it"
    Query (Q)The question the student asks the class"Who am I referring to? Something that can be tired?"
    Key (K)The name tag every student wears, saying what they offer"animal": a living thing · "street": a place
    Value (V)What the student actually says when you listen to themThe information "animal" passes on

    "It" compares its question with every name tag. "animal" matches strongly, "street" weakly. So "it" listens mostly to "animal" and only a little to the rest, and now "it" carries the meaning "the animal".

    The attention formula, one piece at a time

    This is the most important formula in the unit. Learn it exactly:

    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √dₖ ) · V

    Read it from the inside out:

    1. Q·Kᵀ: compare question with name tags. Multiply each word's query with every word's key. A big result means a good match. (This multiply-and-add is the dot product from Unit 0.)
    2. ÷ √dₖ: shrink the scores. dₖ is the length of the key lists (e.g. 64, so √64 = 8). Without shrinking, the scores get so big that learning breaks down. It keeps training stable.
    3. softmax: turn scores into percentages that add up to 100% (Unit 0, Step 0.6).
    4. · V: listen in those proportions. Mix every word's value using the percentages. The result is the word's new, context-aware meaning.
    Worked example: how much does "sat" listen to each word in "The cat sat"?
    1. Match scores (Q·K): The = 2, cat = 6, sat = 3.
    2. Shrink: here dₖ = 4, so divide by √4 = 2. That gives 1, 3, 1.5.
    3. Softmax: e¹ = 2.72, e³ = 20.09, e¹·⁵ = 4.48. The total is 27.29. So the percentages are The 10% · cat 74% · sat 16%.
    4. Result: the new meaning of "sat" is mostly "cat", a little "sat" and a touch of "The", which makes sense because it's the cat that sat.

    Multi-head attention: several discussions at once

    One round of attention can only focus on one kind of relationship. So the model runs several attentions side by side, each called a head. One head might track grammar ("who is the subject?"), another the meaning, and another which earlier word a pronoun refers to. Their results are then joined together.

    Numbers from the original paper512 numbers per word, split across 8 heads, so each head works with 64 numbers (dₖ = 64).

    Steps 4–5: the rest of each block

    • Feed-forward network: after the discussion, each word "thinks alone" by passing through a small neural network. Attention mixes information between words; this step processes each word.
    • Residual connection ("Add"): each word keeps its old numbers and adds the new ones on top, like keeping your notes and adding to them. This lets very deep models train properly.
    • Layer normalisation ("Norm"): rescales the numbers to a sensible range so nothing explodes.

    So one block = Attention → Add & Norm → Feed-forward → Add & Norm. Stack dozens of blocks and you have an LLM.

    § 1.4The Data: What an LLM Reads, and How It's Cleaned

    The big ideaAn LLM learns language by reading a gigantic library of text. But much of the internet is junk, so the text is cleaned first and then chopped into tokens. Clean data matters as much as the amount of data.

    How much does it read?

    Modern LLMs read hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens. A person reading 8 hours a day would need thousands of years to read that much.

    The model doesn't memorise all this text. It learns the patterns of language: grammar, facts, styles of writing. But because it reads almost everything, it also picks up the mistakes and biases in that text.

    Where does the text come from?

    SourceWhat it teaches the model
    Websites (e.g. Common Crawl)Everyday language on every topic
    BooksLong, well-organised writing
    Computer code (e.g. GitHub)Logic, structure, step-by-step reasoning
    WikipediaFacts
    News and research papersFormal writing and up-to-date knowledge

    How is the text cleaned? Five steps

    1. Collect: gather the text from all sources.
    2. Clean: strip out website code, menus, adverts and broken characters, like removing the wrapping paper.
    3. Filter: throw away low-quality text, harmful content and private personal details.
    4. Deduplicate: remove repeated copies. Reading the same page 1,000 times wastes effort and makes the model parrot it word for word.
    5. Tokenize: chop the text into tokens and give each an ID number.

    "Garbage in, garbage out": removing junk and duplicates often improves a model more than adding extra raw text.

    How is the token dictionary built? (Sub-word tokenization)

    Why not use whole words? Because there are too many words, and new ones appear all the time. Why not single letters? Because then sentences become very long. The middle path is sub-words: common words stay whole, and rare words are built from pieces.

    The most common method is BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding). It builds the dictionary like this:

    1. Start with single letters.
    2. Find the pair of neighbours that appears most often in all the text.
    3. Glue that pair into one new piece.
    4. Repeat thousands of times, until the dictionary is the size you want.
    Worked BPE exampleWords in the text: low (5 times), lower (2), newest (6), widest (3).
    The pair "e"+"s" appears 6 + 3 = 9 times (in newest and widest), which is the most, so glue it: "es". (Two other pairs also appear 9 times; any of them could go first.)
    Then "es"+"t" (9 times) gives "est".
    Then "l"+"o" (7 times) gives "lo", then "lo"+"w" gives "low".
    Now a brand-new word like "lowest" can be written as low + est, with no unknown pieces.
    MethodHow it differsUsed by
    BPEGlue the most frequent pair, again and againGPT models
    WordPieceSimilar, but picks the pair that helps the model mostBERT
    SentencePieceWorks straight on raw text (even spaces), so it suits many languagesT5, LLaMA

    Is more data always better? (The Chinchilla lesson)

    In 2022, researchers (Hoffmann et al., the "Chinchilla" paper) found that many big models were under-trained: too many knobs for the amount of text they had read.

    The proofChinchilla (70 billion parameters, read 1.4 trillion tokens) beat Gopher (280 billion parameters, read only 300 billion tokens), even though both used the same amount of computing. A smaller model that reads more beat a bigger model that read less.

    Rule of thumb: about 20 tokens of reading for every parameter. Grow the model and the data together.

    § 1.5Training: How the Model Learns, and What Goes Wrong

    The big ideaTraining is a giant fill-in-the-next-word game. The model guesses, we measure how wrong it was, and we nudge all its knobs a little to be less wrong. This is repeated trillions of times.

    The game the model plays

    1. Take a real sentence: "The Taj Mahal is in ___".
    2. Hide the next word ("Agra").
    3. The model gives every possible word a percentage.
    4. Check: how much did it give to "Agra"?
    5. Nudge the knobs so next time "Agra" gets more.

    No human has to label anything: the text itself contains the answers. This is called self-supervised learning.

    How do we measure "how wrong"? (Loss)

    The wrongness score is called the loss. For LLMs it's the cross-entropy loss: basically, how surprised was the model by the right answer?

    • Gave the right word 90%? Barely surprised, so small loss.
    • Gave the right word 1%? Very surprised, so big loss.
    Loss = − average of log( probability given to the correct word ) Perplexity = e^Loss

    Perplexity is the same idea turned into an easy number: how many words is the model torn between? Lower is better.

    Worked exampleFor three words, the model gave the correct word 0.5, then 0.25, then 0.125.
    Loss = (0.693 + 1.386 + 2.079) ÷ 3 = 1.386
    Perplexity = e^1.386 = 4, meaning the model was as unsure as if it were picking randomly among 4 words.

    How are the knobs nudged? (The optimiser)

    Unit 0 described walking downhill in fog (gradient descent). LLMs use a smarter walker called AdamW. It has three habits:

    • It remembers which way it's been heading, so it doesn't zig-zag.
    • It gives each knob its own step size.
    • The "W" is weight decay: it gently pulls knobs towards zero, which stops the model memorising.

    How fast should it learn? (Learning-rate schedule)

    The learning rate is the size of each step. It changes during training, like a runner:

    1. Warm-up: start with small steps, because at first the model knows nothing and big steps would throw it off.
    2. Peak: take big steps once things are stable, to learn fast.
    3. Decay: slowly shrink the steps to settle carefully into the best spot.

    What goes wrong at huge scale, and the fixes

    ProblemWhat happensFix
    InstabilityThe loss suddenly shoots up mid-trainingWarm-up, gradient clipping (a speed limit on how big one nudge can be), restart from a saved point
    Vanishing / exploding gradientsThe "which way is downhill" signal fades to nothing or blows up in deep modelsResidual connections, layer norm, gradient clipping
    Not enough memoryThe model doesn't fit on one chipMixed precision (store numbers in a shorter 16-bit form), checkpointing (recompute instead of store), splitting across chips
    Cost and timeWeeks of running, millions of dollarsMixed precision (faster), efficient code, right-sized model (Chinchilla)
    Memorising instead of learningThe model repeats training text word for wordWeight decay, dropout (randomly switch off some neurons during practice), deduplication
    Hard to repeat or debugA crash in week 3 loses everythingSave checkpoints often, log everything

    Sharing the work across many chips (parallelism)

    No single chip can hold or train a giant model, so the work is split in three ways. Think of a restaurant kitchen:

    • Data parallel: every cook has the full recipe, but cooks different orders. Afterwards they share what they learned. Makes training faster.
    • Model (tensor) parallel: one giant dish is split, with each cook making part of it. Lets a model that's too big for one chip fit.
    • Pipeline parallel: an assembly line, where chip 1 does the first layers, chip 2 the next, and so on. Keeps every chip busy.

    Big training runs use all three at once.

    § 1.6Hardware: Why LLMs Need Special Chips and So Much Money

    The big ideaAn LLM's work is mostly multiplying big tables of numbers. Special chips called GPUs and TPUs do thousands of these at once. Giant models need thousands of these chips, weeks of time and millions of dollars.

    Why GPUs and TPUs?

    A normal computer chip (a CPU) is like a few brilliant professors: it can do anything, but only a few things at a time. A GPU is like thousands of students who can only do simple sums, but all at the same moment. LLM work is millions of simple multiply-and-add sums, so the students win. (TPUs are Google's own chips built for the same job.)

    Thousands of chips are linked with very fast connections so they act like one giant computer. Often the real limit isn't speed but memory: the whole model has to fit.

    Quick maths you can do in the exam

    Memory to hold the modelEach parameter in 16-bit form takes 2 bytes.
    A 7-billion-parameter model needs 7 × 2 = 14 GB, which fits on one good GPU.
    GPT-3 (175 billion) needs 175 × 2 = 350 GB, which needs several top GPUs just to hold it.
    Training needs about 16 bytes per parameter (it also stores the updates and the optimiser's notes), so GPT-3 needs about 2.8 TB.
    Amount of computingRule: total calculations ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens read.
    GPT-3: 6 × 175 billion × 300 billion ≈ 3.15 × 10²³ calculations ≈ 3,640 petaflop/s-days.

    The real-world costs

    WhatRoughly how muchWhy it matters
    MemoryHundreds of GB for the weights aloneNeeds many GPUs
    Computing timeMillions of GPU-hoursDrives the cost
    MoneyMillions of dollars per big modelOnly a few companies can afford it
    ElectricitySimilar to what many homes use in a yearEnvironmental impact (Unit 5)

    Don't forget daily use (inference). Training happens once, but answering millions of users happens every day, forever. So companies shrink models to run cheaply: quantisation (even shorter numbers, e.g. 4-bit), distillation (training a small model to copy a big one), and mixture-of-experts (only part of the model works on each word).

    The five ingredients of an LLM

    1. Architecture: a deep stack of Transformer blocks.
    2. Data: trillions of clean tokens.
    3. Objective: guess the next token (cross-entropy loss).
    4. Optimisation: AdamW, learning-rate schedule, stabilising fixes.
    5. Compute: thousands of GPUs or TPUs for weeks.

    How does a computer measure "same meaning"? (Cosine similarity)

    Each sentence is turned into an arrow of numbers (an embedding, Unit 0). If two arrows point the same way, the sentences mean similar things. The measure is called cosine similarity: 1 = same meaning, 0 = unrelated.

    cos(θ) = (A · B) / (length of A × length of B)
    Worked exampleA = (1, 2, 2) and B = (2, 1, 2).
    A · B = 1×2 + 2×1 + 2×2 = 8. The length of each arrow is √(1+4+4) = 3.
    cos = 8 ÷ (3 × 3) = 0.89, so very similar.

    This is how smart search works: "forgot my login credentials" matches "reset my password" even though they share no words.

    • Open-book (RAG): search documents first, then answer from them (§ 2.4).

    § 2.2Writing with LLMs: Control, Marking, and Made-up Answers

    The big ideaLLMs can write almost anything, and you can steer how they write just by asking. But their writing is hard to mark automatically, and they sometimes confidently make things up. This is called hallucination.

    What kinds of writing?

    Hallucination: confident but wrong

    ExampleQ: "Who won the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physics?"
    A: "Albert Einstein, for his work on lasers." This is fluent and confident, and completely false.

    • Keep a human checking anything important.

    § 2.3Prompting: Getting the Model to Do What You Want

    The big ideaA prompt is the message you give the model. You "program" an LLM with instructions and examples written in plain language, not code. The model's knobs don't change; only your instructions do.

    Think of a very well-read new intern

    The LLM is like an intern who has read everything but takes you literally. How clearly you explain the task decides how good the work is.

    Three styles of prompt

    StyleWhat you doExample
    Zero-shotJust ask"Translate to French: Good morning"
    Few-shotShow a few examples first, then ask"cat → chat, dog → chien, bird → ?"
    InstructionGive a role, the task, the format and limits"You are a maths tutor. Explain in 2 lines."

    Learning from examples in the message (in-context learning)

    Example prompt Review: "Loved it!" → Positive
    Review: "Total waste of money." → Negative
    Review: "It was fine." → Neutral
    Review: "Best purchase this year." → ?
    The model spots the pattern and answers Positive.

    Picking up a new task just from examples in the prompt is called in-context learning. It was first shown at scale by GPT-3 in 2020 ("Language Models are Few-Shot Learners").

    Few-shot vs fine-tuning: few-shot puts examples in the message. It's instant, free and temporary, and the knobs don't change. Fine-tuning trains the model on many examples, which changes its knobs permanently and costs computing time (Unit 3).

    "Show your working" (chain-of-thought)

    Asking the model to think step by step makes it much better at maths and logic.

    "A shop has 3 boxes of 12 pens and sells 20. How many are left?" Direct answer: often wrong (e.g. "30").
    "Let's think step by step": 3 × 12 = 36 → 36 − 20 = 16 → 16 ✓

    Why it works: the model writes one word at a time, and each word it writes becomes something it can read. Writing the steps gives it room to work the answer out instead of blurting a guess. (Wei et al., 2022.)

    How chat apps organise a conversation

    • System: hidden standing orders from the app maker: "Be polite. Never share private data."
    • User: your messages.
    • Assistant: the model's replies.

    The model has no memory of its own. The app sends the whole conversation again every turn, and that is how it "remembers" what you said.

    § 2.4RAG: Letting the Model Look Things Up

    The big ideaA normal LLM answers from memory, like a closed-book exam. RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) turns it into an open-book exam: before answering, the app finds the right pages in your documents and hands them to the model.

    Why is this needed?

    A model's memory can be wrong, out of date (it only knows what it read before training ended), or simply missing things it never saw, like your college's rules or your company's policies.

    How RAG works, step by step

    Done once, in advance:

    1. Cut your documents into small passages.
    2. Turn each passage into a meaning-arrow (an embedding).
    3. Store them in a special search database (a vector database).

    Every time someone asks a question:

    1. Turn the question into a meaning-arrow too.
    2. Retrieve: find the passages whose arrows are closest (the most similar meaning).
    3. Put those passages into the prompt together with the question.
    4. Generate: the model answers using those passages, ideally saying which document it used.
    ExampleQuestion: "What is the leave policy for interns?"
    The app finds the paragraph from the HR handbook about intern leave, and the model answers from it: "Interns get 1 day of leave per month (HR Handbook, section 4.2)."

    Why RAG is so popular

    • Answers come from your own, current documents.
    • Fewer made-up answers, and people can check the sources.
    • To update its knowledge, just add documents. No retraining needed.

    It's used in customer-support bots, "chat with your PDF" tools and company search.

    The catch: if the search picks the wrong passage, the answer will still be wrong. The model can also only read so much at once (the context window).

    § 2.5Chatbots and Virtual Assistants

    The big ideaOn its own, an LLM just guesses the next word. To make a useful assistant, you surround it with helpers: a memory, a filing cabinet of documents, tools it can use, and safety rules.

    Picture a hotel receptionist

    ReceptionistIn an AI assistantWhat it does
    Their brain and voiceThe LLMUnderstands you and replies fluently
    A notebookMemoryRemembers the conversation and facts about you
    A filing cabinetKnowledge (RAG)Looks things up in official documents
    A phone and computerTools / APIsCalculates, checks bookings, searches the web
    A rulebook and a managerGuardrailsRefuses unsafe requests, protects privacy, hands hard cases to a human

    What happens when you send a message

    1. Understand: work out what you want (your intent) using the conversation so far.
    2. Plan: decide whether to look something up or use a tool.
    3. Generate: write the reply.
    4. Respond: send it to you (after the safety checks).

    How memory works

    ExampleYou: "I bought a Nexon last year."
    Assistant: "Nice! How has the Nexon been?"
    You: "Good. Does it need a service now?"
    Assistant: "Since you bought it a year ago, yes, the first service is due."
    It knew "it" = the Nexon, and remembered "a year ago" from earlier.
    • Short-term memory: the chat history, re-sent to the model every turn.
    • Long-term memory: facts saved in a database and brought back later ("this user owns a Nexon").

    How tools work

    LLMs are bad at exact arithmetic and can't see live information. So when a tool is better, the model asks for the tool instead of guessing.

    ExampleYou: "What's an 18% tip on ₹2,450, and is my table booked for 8 pm?"
    The model writes: calculator(2450 × 0.18) → ₹441, and bookings.lookup() → table 12 at 8 pm.
    The app runs these, gives back the results, and the model replies: "That's a ₹441 tip, and yes, your table is booked for 8 pm."
    This is called tool use or function calling.

    Safety guardrails

    • Input filter: block harmful or off-topic requests before they reach the model.
    • System rules: hidden instructions that set tone and limits.
    • Output checks: screen replies for private data or harmful content.
    • Grounding: answer only from approved documents.
    • Human hand-off: pass serious cases to a real person.
    • Logging: keep records to spot and fix failures.

    § 2.6Translation

    The big ideaMachine translation went from word-by-word phrasebooks, to whole sentences, to LLMs that understand whole context and tone. BLEU is the usual way to mark translations, but it only counts matching words.

    Three generations

    GenerationHow it works, simplyQuality
    Statistical (SMT), 2000sTranslate chunk by chunk using statistics from millions of translated sentence pairsStiff and literal, like a phrasebook
    Neural (NMT), ~2016A neural network reads the whole sentence, then writes the translation (Google Translate switched in 2016)Much smoother
    LLM, 2022+One model that knows many languages translates straight awayKeeps tone, idioms and context
    IdiomsEnglish: "Could you give me a hand?"
    LLM, into Hindi: "क्या आप मेरी मदद कर सकते हैं?" ("Can you help me?")
    Not the literal "give me your hand": the LLM translates the meaning.

    Weak spots: languages with little text online (many Indian languages) get weaker translations. Projects like AI4Bharat's IndicTrans2 and the government's Bhashini are working on this. The model can also produce a confident mistranslation.

    Marking a translation with BLEU

    BLEU compares the machine's sentence with a human answer key. It counts matching single words, then matching pairs, triples and groups of four, and combines them into a score from 0 (nothing matches) to 1 (identical).

    Worked exampleAnswer key: "The cat sat on the mat." Machine: "The cat sat on a mat."
    Single words matching: 5 of 6 · Pairs: 3 of 5 · Triples: 2 of 4 · Groups of four: 1 of 3.
    BLEU = (5/6 × 3/5 × 2/4 × 1/3)^(¼) ≈ 0.54 (the sentences are the same length, so there is no length penalty).

    BLEU's weakness: a perfect translation in different words ("The feline rested on the rug") scores very low. BLEU has no idea about meaning or grammar, so humans still check.

    § 2.7Summarisation

    The big ideaThere are two ways to shorten text: copy the important sentences (extractive) or rewrite it in your own words (abstractive). LLMs do the second. For very long documents, they summarise piece by piece.

    Highlighter vs your own notes

    Extractive: the highlighterAbstractive: your own notes
    HowPicks the key sentences and copies them exactlyUnderstands the text and writes a new, short version
    Safe from errors?Yes: nothing is made upMust be checked: it can slip in wrong facts
    Reads well?Can feel choppySmooth and short
    Choose it when…Exact wording matters (legal, compliance)Readability matters (news, meeting notes, briefs)

    What if the document is too long?

    A model can only read so much at once (its context window). The trick is map-reduce, like splitting a thick book among friends:

    1. Split the document into chunks.
    2. Map: summarise each chunk separately.
    3. Combine the mini-summaries.
    4. Reduce: summarise the combined summaries into one final summary.

    Where it's used: meeting and call transcripts, legal contracts, research papers. Risks: details get lost between chunks, and invented facts can creep in, so check against the source. Summaries are marked with ROUGE plus human review.

    § 2.8Content Creation and Code

    The big ideaLLMs can produce a first draft of almost anything in seconds: emails, articles, adverts, quizzes, tables, even code. Treat it as a fast first draft. You are the editor who checks it and takes responsibility.

    What it can create

    • Writing: emails, blogs, reports, stories.
    • Code: functions and tests, plus explaining code and finding bugs.
    • Marketing: adverts, product descriptions.
    • Structured data: tables, JSON.
    • Learning material: quizzes, flashcards, notes.
    • Rewriting: simpler, longer, or in a different tone.
    Code exampleAsk: "Write a Python function that returns the factorial of n."
    def factorial(n): if n <= 1: return 1 return n * factorial(n - 1)
    It works, but notice it silently accepts negative numbers. Always test generated code.
    Structured output exampleAsk: "Pull out name, role and company as JSON: Ada Lovelace is an analyst at Analytical Co."
    Answer: {"name": "Ada Lovelace", "role": "Analyst", "company": "Analytical Co."}
    This is how LLMs plug into other software.

    The risks

    • Made-up facts and fake references.
    • Bias copied from its training text.
    • Plagiarism and copyright problems (see RM Unit 2).
    • Convincing false information produced at huge scale.
    • Over-relying on it without checking.

    § 2.9Choosing the Right Approach, and Using LLMs Responsibly

    The big ideaThere are three ways to make an LLM do your job. Try them in this order: ask well (prompting) → give it documents (RAG) → train it further (fine-tuning), and only fine-tune if you really must.

    Think of hiring a smart graduate

    ApproachLike…Best whenCost
    PromptingGiving clear instructionsMost tasksFree, instant
    RAGHanding them the company handbookThe job needs specific or up-to-date factsMedium
    Fine-tuningSending them on a training course (changes the knobs)You need a fixed style or deep expertise in a narrow area (Unit 3)Expensive

    Limits to always remember

    • Not always right: it can be confidently wrong.
    • Bias: it can repeat and amplify unfair patterns.
    • Privacy: be careful with personal data (India's DPDP Act 2023).
    • No real understanding: it predicts patterns; it doesn't "know" like a person.
    • Cost and speed: big models are slow and expensive to run.
    • Responsibility: a human is responsible for what it produces.

    Flashcards · Unit Two

    The professor's six Unit 2 self-checks, plus cards that go back over Unit 1 (↺).

    Self-check 1: give one NLU task and one NLG task. What's the difference?
    NLU: sentiment analysis (reads text, gives a label). NLG: summarising (writes new text). NLU reads and decides; NLG writes.
    Self-check 2: few-shot prompting vs fine-tuning?
    Few-shot: examples go in the message, the knobs don't change, it's instant and temporary. Fine-tuning: the model is trained further, its knobs change permanently, and it costs computing time.
    Self-check 3: what is RAG, and how does it reduce made-up answers?
    Retrieval-Augmented Generation: search your documents, put the best passages in the prompt, then answer from them. It's an open-book exam: answers are based on real sources that can be checked.
    Self-check 4: what turns an LLM into a chatbot?
    Memory (the chat history plus saved facts) + knowledge (RAG) + tools (function calling) + the system/user/assistant chat format + safety guardrails.
    Self-check 5: why can a good translation get a low BLEU score?
    BLEU only counts matching words with one answer key. A correct translation in different words scores low, because BLEU ignores meaning.
    Self-check 6: when would you choose abstractive over extractive summaries?
    When readability matters (news, meeting notes, briefs). Choose extractive when exact wording matters (legal, compliance).
    Name five NLU (reading) jobs.
    Classification, sentiment, NER (finding names), intent + slots, semantic similarity (also question answering and entailment).
    What is hallucination, and how do apps reduce it?
    Fluent, confident but false output, because the model is trained to sound likely, not to be checked. Reduce it with RAG, citing sources, allowing "I don't know", tools, and human checks.
    What is chain-of-thought prompting?
    Asking the model to "think step by step". It writes out the steps (3 × 12 = 36; 36 − 20 = 16), which makes maths and logic answers much more reliable.
    Cosine similarity of (1, 2, 2) and (2, 1, 2)?
    Dot product = 8; each length = 3; cos = 8 ÷ 9 = 0.89 (very similar).
    BLEU for "The cat sat on a mat" against "The cat sat on the mat"?
    Matches: words 5/6, pairs 3/5, triples 2/4, fours 1/3, so BLEU ≈ 0.54.
    SMT vs NMT vs LLM translation?
    SMT (2000s): chunk by chunk from statistics, literal. NMT (~2016): neural, whole sentences, smoother. LLM (2022+): one multilingual model, keeps tone and idioms.
    How does map-reduce summarise a very long document?
    Split into chunks → summarise each (map) → combine → summarise the summaries (reduce).
    Which Transformer type suits reading, and which suits writing?
    Encoder-only (BERT) for reading/understanding. Decoder-only (GPT) for writing. Encoder–decoder (T5) for translation and summaries.
    Creative writing vs factual answers: which temperature?
    Creative writing: higher temperature (more variety). Facts, JSON or code: low temperature (safe and predictable).
    Why do embeddings make smart search possible?
    Embeddings place similar meanings close together, so the search finds passages that mean the same as the question even without shared words. This is the retrieval step of RAG.
